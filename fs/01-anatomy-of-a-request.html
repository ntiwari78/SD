Anatomy of a Request

You cannot debug a system whose shape you cannot picture. Before any React or Spring Boot, this stage builds one durable mental model: what physically happens between a click and a rendered row — and where the milliseconds go.

01 / THE PATH

One request, end to end

A student clicks “My scholarships”. Roughly 300 milliseconds later a table appears. What actually happened?

The honest answer is that about eighteen distinct things happened, on at least five machines, and only two of them were your code. Most engineers can name the pieces. Far fewer can say, when a page takes 1.4 seconds, which piece owns the second.

So start here, with a model you can run. The simulator below walks a real navigation to https://app.scholarships.org/students?cohort=2026 and then the API call that fills the table. Every number is a plausible production number, not a placeholder — change a condition and watch the shape of the bar chart change with it.

What the simulator is showing you

Three lessons are hiding in those toggles, and they are the reason this stage exists.

  1. Round trips dominate. Turn distance up to cross-continent and watch the connection setup swamp everything your code does. Your database query is 18 ms. Three round trips at 180 ms are 540. You cannot optimise a query out of a geography problem — you move the compute, or you stop making round trips.
  2. Layers you did not write are on the critical path. DNS, TLS, the CDN, the preflight. None of them appear in your code, all of them appear in the user's stopwatch.
  3. The N+1 is invisible until you look. Toggle it on. One extra JPA relationship, lazily loaded inside a loop over forty rows, and the data tier goes from a rounding error to the largest bar on the chart. Stage 8 is where you learn to see this coming; this is where you learn what it costs.

The rule this stage is really teaching

Find the bottleneck before you touch anything. Every optimisation you make without a measurement is a guess that adds complexity, and roughly two-thirds of guesses are wrong. The waterfall above is the shape of the measurement; Chrome DevTools' Network panel and a distributed trace are the real instruments — Stages 11 and 12.

Experiment

Set distance to cross-continent, turn on cold DNS and CDN miss, and turn Redis cache hit off. Now ask yourself which single change buys the most: a CDN region closer to the user, connection reuse, or a faster query? Then verify against the chart. The answer is almost never the one engineers reach for first.

Challenge

Open any real site with DevTools → Network, hard-reload, and identify the same phases in the timing breakdown of the document request: Queueing, Stalled, DNS Lookup, Initial connection, SSL, Request sent, Waiting (TTFB), Content Download. 02 / RESOLUTION

DNS, and why the first request is different

The browser has a hostname and needs an IP address. Who does it ask, and how many times?

DNS is a distributed, aggressively cached, hierarchical lookup. The word that matters there is cached: in steady state DNS costs nothing, which is exactly why it surprises people when it costs 120 ms. Every layer below can answer, and the walk stops at the first one that can.

Resolver ladder

Interactive · step through

Under the hood

Records have a TTL, which is a promise about staleness, not a guarantee — resolvers routinely honour their own minimums. That has a practical consequence you will meet in Stage 14: DNS is not a deployment mechanism. Changing a record does not move traffic promptly, because you do not control the caches. Traffic shifting belongs to a load balancer or an Azure Front Door routing rule, where the change is immediate and reversible.

The other practical consequence is preconnect. If your React app is served from one origin and calls an API on another, the API's DNS lookup, TCP connect and TLS handshake all happen at the moment of the first fetch — inside the user's wait. One line in your index.html moves that cost earlier:

<link rel="preconnect" href="https://api.scholarships.org" crossorigin>
✕“I changed the DNS record, so traffic has moved.”

It has moved for resolvers whose TTL has expired, which is a fraction of them, arriving over hours. Some corporate resolvers pin records far longer than your TTL. Design rollbacks that do not depend on DNS propagation.

✕“DNS is fast, it doesn't matter.”

Warm, it is free. Cold — a first-time visitor, a new subdomain, a mobile network that just changed towers — it is a full walk of the hierarchy. Splitting your app across many hostnames multiplies that cost, which is one of several reasons the “shard across four CDN domains” trick from the HTTP/1.1 era is now an anti-pattern.

03 / TRANSPORT

TCP, TLS, and the tyranny of the round trip

Before a single byte of your JSON moves, how many times has the packet crossed the ocean?

TCP gives you an ordered, reliable byte stream, and charges one round trip to set it up. TLS gives you confidentiality, integrity and server identity, and charges one or two more depending on version. None of that is optional, and none of it is your application's code — but all of it is on the user's clock, and it is why connection reuse is one of the highest-leverage performance ideas in the stack.

Handshake cost calculator

Interactive · model

What this means for your architecture

  • Keep-alive is not a micro-optimisation. A connection pool on the server side (HikariCP to PostgreSQL) and connection reuse on the client side are the same idea applied at two tiers: setup is expensive, so amortise it.
  • HTTP/2 removed the reason to shard domains. One connection, many multiplexed streams. Under HTTP/1.1 browsers opened about six connections per origin and everything else queued — which is why the old advice was to spread assets across hostnames, and why that advice is now actively harmful.
  • HTTP/3 runs over QUIC, on UDP. It folds the transport and cryptographic handshakes together and removes TCP's head-of-line blocking, so one lost packet no longer stalls every stream. On a flaky mobile network this is the difference you feel.

Architecture connection

In the capstone, the React bundle is served from Azure Front Door at the edge (short path, cached, HTTP/3) while the API is a Spring Boot container in one region (long path, uncacheable). That asymmetry is the whole reason for splitting them: static bytes get to be near the user, dynamic bytes have to be near the data. Every CDN decision you make later is an argument about which of those two a given response is.

04 / PROTOCOL

HTTP is a contract, not a transport detail

Why is it wrong to expose “create a student” as POST /createStudent — and does it actually matter?

It matters because HTTP methods and status codes are not naming conventions. They are promises, and an entire layer of infrastructure you do not control — browsers, proxies, CDNs, retry libraries, service meshes — reads those promises and acts on them without asking you. Get them wrong and something between you and the user will do the wrong thing confidently.

Status codes, as a triage tool

    
When you see a failure, the first digit tells you whose problem it is before you read a single log line. 4xx is the client's fault — malformed, unauthenticated, forbidden, gone. 5xx is yours — the server accepted a legal request and failed to honour it. That single distinction saves more debugging time than any tool.

    
    
      
  • 400 the body or parameters are invalid · 422 syntactically fine, semantically rejected
    • 
      
  • 401 we do not know who you are — authentication · 403 we know, and you may not — authorization
    • 
      
  • 404 no such resource — also, deliberately, what a good API returns instead of 403 when the existence of the record is itself confidential
    • 
      
  • 409 conflict, usually an optimistic-locking version clash · 429 rate limited, and the response should carry Retry-After
    • 
      
  • 500 unhandled · 502/503/504 the gateway could not reach, or waited too long for, the thing behind it
    • 
    

  


  

    
✕Returning 200 OK with {"error": "not found"} in the body
      
Every retry policy, alerting rule, CDN, and API client in the world now believes this request succeeded. Your error rate dashboard reads zero during an outage. The status line is machine-readable for a reason; the body is for humans and for detail.

    
✕Using GET /students/42/delete
      
A GET is safe and prefetchable. A browser's link prefetcher, a crawler, a corporate proxy that pre-warms links, or a user's “open all in tabs” will delete your data. This is not hypothetical — it is how a well-known 2006 outage of Google Web Accelerator deleted a great deal of other people's content.

    
✕Inventing your own error shape per endpoint
      
Your React error handler then needs a branch per endpoint. Pick one envelope and enforce it in a single @RestControllerAdvice. RFC 9457 Problem Details is the standard shape, and Spring Framework 6+ supports it natively via ProblemDetail. Stage 7.

05 / ORIGINS
CORS: the error everyone hits on day one

  
Your React dev server is on :5173, Spring Boot is on :8080, and the browser refuses to show you the response. Why is the browser stopping you?

  

    
Here is the thing almost everyone gets backwards. CORS is not a server security feature. The server already sent the response — you can see it in the Network tab, status 200, body intact. The browser received it and then refused to hand it to your JavaScript, because the page's origin does not match the response's origin and the server did not explicitly opt in.

    
The rule being enforced is the same-origin policy, and it is the reason a random page cannot read your bank balance using your logged-in cookies. CORS is the controlled exception to it. Two consequences follow immediately: disabling CORS in the browser is not a fix, and CORS protects users, not your server — anything with a shell can call your API regardless.

    
Two origins match only if scheme, host and port are all identical. http://localhost:5173 and http://localhost:8080 are different origins. So are https://app.example.org and https://api.example.org.

The preflight rule, precisely

    
The browser skips the preflight only for a simple request: method is GET, HEAD or POST; every header is on the CORS-safelist; and if Content-Type is present it is one of application/x-www-form-urlencoded, multipart/form-data or text/plain. Notice what is missing from that list: application/json. Which is why essentially every real API call from a React app triggers a preflight, and why an Authorization: Bearer … header does too.

    
A preflight is an extra OPTIONS round trip before the real one — the second bar you saw in the simulator in Module 1. Cache it with Access-Control-Max-Age and the cost disappears for subsequent calls.

  


  

    
✕Combining allowedOrigins("*") with allowCredentials(true)
      
The specification forbids it, and Spring will throw at startup rather than let you do it. A wildcard origin with credentials would mean “any site on the internet may make authenticated requests as this user”. Use allowedOriginPatterns or an explicit list.

    
✕Fixing CORS with a browser extension or --disable-web-security
      
You have disabled the check on exactly one machine. Every user still fails. Worse, you have now removed the protection that keeps other sites out of your session on that machine. Fix it on the server, or use the Vite dev proxy so that in development everything is same-origin.

    
✕Configuring CORS on the controller while Spring Security rejects the preflight
      
The OPTIONS preflight carries no credentials — by design. If your security filter chain requires authentication for every request, it rejects the preflight with a 401 before your @CrossOrigin annotation is ever consulted, and the browser reports a CORS error that has nothing to do with your CORS config. Register CORS with Spring Security (http.cors(Customizer.withDefaults())) so the CorsFilter runs early in the chain. This is the single most common “my CORS config does nothing” bug.

  


  
Experiment

    
In the sandbox, set method POST, Content-Type text/plain, no auth header — note that no preflight fires. Now change Content-Type to application/json and nothing else. One field, one extra round trip. That is the whole rule in one keystroke.

Reading the result honestly

    
Nothing here is free. Sticky sessions work until an instance dies or you deploy, and they wreck even load distribution. Redis-backed sessions scale horizontally and survive restarts, at the price of a network hop on every request and a new piece of infrastructure that can itself fail. JWTs remove the lookup entirely — and remove your ability to revoke, which is why access tokens must be short-lived and paired with refresh tokens you can revoke.

    
The trap is treating “stateless” as automatically superior. Stateless means the server holds no per-user state between requests. It does not mean simple. You have moved state to the client, and now you own token expiry, rotation, revocation, clock skew and key management. Sessions in Redis are a perfectly respectable production answer, and for an application with an admin who must be able to eject a user immediately — like a platform holding minors' records — it may be the better one.

The recommendation this curriculum makes — and why

    
For the capstone: short-lived access token in memory, refresh token in an HttpOnly; Secure; SameSite=Strict cookie. This is an architectural recommendation, not a specification requirement, and reasonable engineers disagree. The reasoning: a token in localStorage is readable by any injected script, and a platform holding students' documents cannot accept “one XSS equals total account takeover”. An HttpOnly cookie is not readable by JavaScript at all; SameSite=Strict closes the CSRF hole that cookies otherwise open. You will build exactly this in Stage 9 — including the part the Eazy Bytes deck never shows you, which is Spring Boot actually issuing the token.

  


  
Architecture connection

    
Stateless services are what make Stage 13's containers and Stage 14's rolling deployments boring. If any instance can serve any request, you can kill one mid-deploy and nobody notices. The moment a request must return to a specific instance, every operational procedure you have gets harder. That is the real payoff — not performance.

07 / IDENTITY
Authentication is not authorization

  
Same user, same token, same endpoint — one request returns 200 and the next returns 403. What changed?

  

    
Two questions, asked in order, by two different parts of your system.

    
    
      
  • Authentication — who are you? Answered once, at the edge of the request, by verifying a credential: a password against a BCrypt hash, a signature on a token, a session cookie against a store. Failure is 401 Unauthorized, and the name is a historical misnomer — it means unauthenticated.
    • 
      
  • Authorization — may you do this, to this? Answered at every protected operation, against roles, permissions and often the specific record. Failure is 403 Forbidden.
    • 
    

    
The distinction matters because the failures have opposite remedies. A 401 means get a new credential — your React client should refresh the token or redirect to login. A 403 means stop asking — refreshing changes nothing, and a client that retries on 403 will loop forever.

    
The part that bites in real systems

    
Role checks are the easy half. The half that produces breaches is object-level authorization: the NGO reviewer is legitimately authenticated, legitimately holds the REVIEWER role, and requests GET /api/v1/applications/8891 — an application belonging to a different NGO. Every role check passes. The data still leaks. OWASP ranks this class of flaw first in its API Top 10, and the only fix is to check ownership against the authenticated principal at the service layer, on every single read and write.

    
// Not enough — the role is right, the record may not be
@PreAuthorize("hasRole('REVIEWER')")
Application get(Long id) { ... }

// The check that actually protects the record
@PreAuthorize("hasRole('REVIEWER') and @acl.canReview(#id, authentication)")
Application get(Long id) { ... }
