It Was Slow at 14:32

That is the whole subject, phrased as a support ticket. Your deck covers Actuator's endpoints and Logback's levels competently, and then stops — no Micrometer, no Prometheus, no tracing, no correlation IDs, no alerting, and no notion of what you would actually do at 3am. Stage 11 assumed a number was available at every turn. This stage is where those numbers come from, and it is organised around a single test: can you answer the person who filed the ticket?

01 / THE TICKET

Seven questions, and what it takes to answer each

“The reviewer dashboard was really slow yesterday afternoon, around half past two.” One sentence, no trace ID, no screenshot, and a user who has already closed the tab.

Observability is usually defined abstractly — the property of being able to understand a system's internal state from its outputs — and the definition is correct and completely unhelpful for deciding what to build. So here is the operational version. A system is observable to the extent that it can answer questions you did not anticipate, after the fact, about a specific request that has already finished.

Below are the seven questions this one ticket actually contains. Switch on the telemetry you have and see which of them you can answer.

What the pattern shows

  1. Log levels are not observability. Turning DEBUG on for a package answers questions you thought of in advance, in a place you already suspected, on a day when the problem happens again. It answers none of the seven.
  2. Metrics tell you whether, traces tell you where, logs tell you why. They are not three implementations of the same idea and they are not substitutes; each answers a class of question the others structurally cannot.
  3. The correlation ID is the cheapest thing on the list and it unlocks the most. One value, propagated and logged, converts “forty instances of grep” into one query — and it is a handful of configuration lines in Spring Boot.
  4. Two of the seven need something you must decide in advance: whether the trace for a specific slow request was kept at all. That is the sampling question, and it is module 06 — the one place where a decision made months ago determines whether today's incident is answerable.

The test to apply to any observability proposal

Before adding a dashboard, a log line or a metric, name the question it answers and the moment you will ask it. Telemetry that no question needs is cost with a maintenance burden, and it is where most observability spend goes — dashboards nobody opens, logs nobody greps, metrics with no alert and no consumer. The inverse test is more useful still: after every incident, write down which question was hard to answer, and add exactly the telemetry that would have answered it. — My recommendation; the SRE literature calls the second half a blameless postmortem action item, and it is the only reliable way I know to grow telemetry that earns its cost.

02 / CARDINALITY

The number that decides which signal a fact belongs in

Adding userId to a metric tag is one word. It is also, on a dashboard with ten thousand users, about two hundred thousand time series and a Prometheus that stops responding.

Almost every real observability mistake is the same mistake: putting a high-cardinality fact on a metric. It is easy to make because the code looks reasonable, the local test passes with three users, and the failure arrives weeks later as a memory graph that only goes up.

The arithmetic is worth internalising because it is multiplicative. A metric's cost is the product of the distinct values of every tag on it — each combination is a separate time series, each series carries its own samples, and nothing prunes them. Build a tag set and watch it.

Where the high-cardinality facts should go instead

They are not forbidden — they are enormously valuable, and they belong on a span, where they cost nothing extra. A span already exists per request; adding user.id, application.id and the full URL to it adds bytes to one record rather than a new time series to a database that keeps them forever. Micrometer's Observation API makes the distinction explicit and it is worth using deliberately:

Observation.createNotStarted("application.submit", registry)
    .lowCardinalityKeyValue("outcome", decision.name())      // → metrics AND traces
    .lowCardinalityKeyValue("scholarship.type", type.name())
    .highCardinalityKeyValue("application.id", String.valueOf(id))   // → traces only
    .highCardinalityKeyValue("user.id", principal.publicId())
    .observe(() -> service.submit(command));

Two keys, two destinations, one call. The low-cardinality pair becomes tags on a timer you can graph and alert on; the high-cardinality pair becomes span attributes you can search when you have a specific incident. This is the single most useful thing the Observation API does, and it is the reason to reach for it rather than for a bare Timer.

The percentile trap, which is the same mistake wearing different clothes

Micrometer offers two ways to get percentiles out of a Timer, and they are not interchangeable.

  • publishPercentiles(0.5, 0.95, 0.99) computes the approximations inside your application and publishes them as values. Micrometer's documentation is blunt about the consequence: these are not aggregable across dimensions. Three instances each publishing their own p95 gives you three numbers you cannot combine — the mean of three p95s is not the fleet's p95, and no amount of dashboard arithmetic recovers it.
  • publishPercentileHistogram() publishes buckets. The backend sums bucket counts across instances and derives the percentile from the aggregate, which is correct. For Prometheus this is the right choice, and it is what makes histogram_quantile() meaningful.

The teaching point generalises past Micrometer: a percentile is not a number you can average. Anything that pre-computes one at the edge has destroyed the information needed to compute it correctly later. This is exactly Stage 11's warning about averaging percentiles, arriving here as a configuration choice with a right answer.

The eight meter types, and picking one

Micrometer has Counter, Gauge, Timer, DistributionSummary, LongTaskTimer, FunctionCounter, FunctionTimer and TimeGauge. Three decisions cover almost every case. Something that only increases and that you will want a rate of — a Counter. Something with a current value that can go down — a Gauge, and note that gauges are sampled rather than recorded, so a gauge on a value that spikes between scrapes will miss the spike. Something with a duration — a Timer, and if the operation can run for minutes, a LongTaskTimer, which reports work in progress rather than only on completion. That last one is the answer to “the nightly export has been running for two hours and no metric shows it”, which an ordinary Timer genuinely cannot tell you.

Micrometer also ships a HighCardinalityTagsDetector that watches for a meter name accumulating series past a threshold and warns. Turn it on in a pre-production environment: it converts a class of outage into a log line.

03 / CONTEXT

One identifier, all the way down

A trace ID that survives the browser, the gateway, three thread hops, a message queue and a database call is worth more than any dashboard. Each of those boundaries is a place it can be silently dropped.

The mechanism is a single HTTP header, standardised by the W3C so that instrumentation from different vendors interoperates. It looks like this, and every field is doing something:

Trace Context is a W3C Recommendation and has been since 2021, which is why an OpenTelemetry-instrumented React app, a Spring Boot service and a managed gateway from a different vendor all agree about what to read and write. A Level 2 draft adds a flag indicating that the trace ID's rightmost bytes are random, which lets a sampler decide consistently without hashing. The companion Baggage specification — for propagating arbitrary key-value pairs alongside the trace — is a Candidate Recommendation rather than a Recommendation, so treat it as stable-in-practice but not finished, and never put anything sensitive in it: baggage travels to every downstream service, including ones you do not own.

Getting the trace ID into every log line

    
This is the highest-value configuration in the whole stage and it is two properties. Spring Boot puts traceId and spanId into the logging MDC when tracing is enabled, and logging.pattern.correlation renders them into every line:

  



# Correlation in plain-text logs
logging.pattern.correlation=[${spring.application.name:},%X{traceId:-},%X{spanId:-}]
logging.include-application-name=false

# Or skip the pattern entirely and emit structured logs — Spring Boot 3.4+
logging.structured.format.console=ecs        # or: logstash, gelf
logging.structured.format.file=ecs
logging.structured.ecs.service.name=scholarships-api
logging.structured.ecs.service.environment=production


  

    

      
A naming mismatch that costs an afternoon

      
Micrometer Tracing writes the MDC keys as traceId and spanId. The OpenTelemetry Java agent writes them as trace_id, span_id and trace_flags. They are the same values under different names, so a log pattern written for one renders empty under the other — with no error, because a missing MDC key is not an error. If your correlation column is blank after switching instrumentation approaches, this is why. It is also a small argument for the Spring-native path: one set of names, configured once.

    


    
Two paths into OpenTelemetry, and which one to take

    
You can instrument a Spring Boot application two ways, and this changed recently enough that most guides describe only the older one.

    
    
      
  • The OpenTelemetry Java agent — a -javaagent that instruments bytecode at load time. It requires no code changes and covers an enormous number of libraries. Its costs: the agent version has to be kept compatible, it does not work with GraalVM native images, and it can conflict with other agents.
    • 
      
  • spring-boot-starter-opentelemetry — a starter, new in Spring Boot 4, that wires Micrometer's Observation API to the OpenTelemetry SDK and OTLP export. No agent, works with native images, and the instrumentation is the same Observation API you use for your own business spans. The Spring team explicitly prefers this, and so do I for a greenfield service; the agent remains the right answer for an application you cannot rebuild or whose libraries Spring does not instrument.
    • 
    

    
Either way the wire format is OTLP and the backend is your choice, which is the actual point of OpenTelemetry: the instrumentation in your code is not a commitment to a vendor.

04 / SIGNALS
Alert on what users feel, not on what servers do

  
CPU at 90% is not an incident. CPU at 90% with latency unchanged is a well-utilised machine. The page that wakes someone at 3am should be about a person having a bad time.


  

    
Three published frameworks answer “what should I measure”, and they are complementary rather than competing because they are aimed at different things.

    
    
      
  • The Four Golden Signals (Google's SRE book): latency, traffic, errors, saturation. The general-purpose set, and the one to start from for a service.
    • 
      
  • RED (Tom Wilkie): rate, errors, duration — the golden signals minus saturation, aimed squarely at request-driven services. Wilkie's argument for dropping saturation is worth quoting: the USE method “doesn't really apply to services; it applies to hardware, network disks, things like this.”
    • 
      
  • USE (Brendan Gregg): for every resource, utilisation, saturation, errors. Aimed at things with a capacity — CPU, memory, disks, connection pools, thread pools.
    • 
    

    
So: RED for your endpoints, USE for your resources, and the difference between them is the difference between a symptom and a cause. Below, six incidents and eleven candidate metrics. Find out which ones would have told you something.

The rule that falls out of the matrix

    
Page on symptoms. Dashboard on causes. An alert should fire when users are experiencing something bad — latency past your objective, errors above budget, a queue growing without bound — because those are the conditions that are always worth waking someone for, whatever caused them. Resource metrics belong on the dashboard you open after the page, to work out why.

    
The practical consequence is that you need far fewer alerts than teams typically have. A service with four symptom alerts and thirty dashboard panels is in much better shape than one with thirty alerts, because thirty alerts guarantee that most of them are ignored — and an ignored alert is worse than a missing one, since it also trains people to ignore the next.


    
What Actuator gives you for free, and what to expose

    
Spring Boot's Actuator publishes a large set of metrics without any code: http.server.requests with URI, method, status and outcome tags; JVM memory, threads and GC; the HikariCP metrics from Stage 11's module 06; cache statistics; and Tomcat's thread pool. That covers RED for every endpoint and USE for most of your resources before you write a line.

    
One security note that Stage 9 makes non-negotiable: only health is exposed over HTTP by default, and that default is correct. /actuator/env, /actuator/configprops and /actuator/heapdump will hand an attacker your configuration and your heap. Expose deliberately, on a separate management port, behind network policy — never by setting management.endpoints.web.exposure.include=* because a tutorial did.

  



# Expose what you need and nothing else, on a port that is not public.
management.server.port=9090
management.endpoints.web.exposure.include=health,info,prometheus
management.endpoint.health.show-details=when-authorized
management.endpoint.health.probes.enabled=true      # liveness + readiness for Kubernetes

# Percentile histograms — buckets, aggregable across instances (module 02)
management.metrics.distribution.percentiles-histogram.http.server.requests=true
management.metrics.distribution.slo.http.server.requests=100ms,250ms,500ms,1s,2s

# Common tags, so every metric carries where it came from
management.observations.key-values.service=scholarships-api
management.observations.key-values.environment=${ENVIRONMENT:local}


  

    
The slo line deserves attention: it adds explicit histogram buckets at boundaries you care about, which makes “what fraction of requests were under 250 ms” a cheap, exact query rather than an interpolation. Choose those boundaries to match the objectives you will define in module 08, and the two halves of this stage connect.


    
Health checks that mean something

    
A health endpoint that returns UP whenever the process is running is a liveness check, and it is worth having. It is not a readiness check, and confusing the two produces the specific failure where a load balancer sends traffic to an instance whose database connection is dead. Liveness answers “should this be restarted”; readiness answers “should this receive traffic”, and a dependency being down means the second and not the first — restarting the process will not fix your database.

05 / TRACES
Reading a waterfall

  
A trace is not a log with better formatting. It is a tree of timed spans, and its shape tells you things no amount of reading the code will.


  

    
Each span has a name, a start, a duration, a parent, and a bag of attributes. That is nearly all there is to the data model, and almost every diagnostic use follows from the shape rather than from any individual span: work that repeats, work that is sequential when it could be parallel, work that has no span at all, and the one chain of spans that actually determines the response time — the critical path.

Four silhouettes worth recognising instantly

    
    
      
  • A picket fence — dozens or hundreds of short sibling spans with the same name. Always an N+1, whether in the ORM (Stage 8) or in a loop of HTTP calls. The trace makes it visible in a way a query log does not, because you can see it happened inside one request.
    • 
      
  • A staircase — spans that start one after another when nothing forces the order. Sequential calls that could be concurrent. Stage 11's fan-out arithmetic says this is also a tail-latency problem: sequential calls add their latencies while parallel ones take the maximum.
    • 
      
  • A gap — dead time inside a parent span with no child covering it. Either something is not instrumented, or the request is waiting for a resource: a connection from the pool, a lock, a thread. Both cases matter and both are invisible without the parent span to compare against.
    • 
      
  • A long tail on one child — a single slow dependency. The easy case, and the one people expect; worth noticing that it is the least common of the four in practice.
    • 
    


    
Span events and exceptions

    
A span can carry timestamped events as well as attributes, which is where “retry attempt 2” and “cache miss” belong — they are moments inside the span, not properties of it. Recorded exceptions are events too, which means a stack trace can live on the span that failed rather than in a log line you then have to correlate. Use it: an exception on the span is findable by trace ID, by service, and by attribute, which a log line is only if you did everything in module 03 correctly.


    
Instrumenting your own work

  



@Service
class ApplicationSubmissionService {

    private final ObservationRegistry registry;

    public SubmissionResult submit(SubmitCommand cmd) {
        return Observation.createNotStarted("application.submit", registry)
            .lowCardinalityKeyValue("scholarship.type", cmd.type().name())
            .highCardinalityKeyValue("application.id", String.valueOf(cmd.applicationId()))
            .observe(() -> {
                var decision = eligibility.evaluate(cmd);       // its own child span
                var saved = repository.save(cmd.toEntity(decision));
                events.publishAfterCommit(new ApplicationSubmitted(saved.getId()));
                return new SubmissionResult(saved.getId(), decision);
            });
    }
}

// Or, for the common case, the annotation — needs an ObservedAspect bean:
@Observed(name = "eligibility.evaluate", contextualName = "evaluate-eligibility")
public EligibilityDecision evaluate(Application app) { … }


  

    
One span per meaningful business operation, not one per method. The value of a trace comes from its shape being legible, and a trace with four hundred spans of framework plumbing is as unreadable as one with none. A good rule: instrument the operations you would name in a sentence describing what the request does.


    

      
Experiment

      
Run your capstone locally with an OTLP collector and a trace UI — Jaeger or Grafana Tempo, both a single container — and submit one application. Then look at the trace without reading any code and try to describe what the request did. Where the trace is unreadable, either the naming is wrong or you have instrumented at the wrong granularity, and both are much cheaper to fix now than during an incident. Then deliberately introduce Stage 8's N+1 and look at the shape it makes.

06 / SAMPLING
The decision that determines whether module 01 has an answer

  
At 10% head sampling, the specific slow request your user is asking about was thrown away before it finished. Nine times out of ten.


  

    
Storing every span from every request is usually unaffordable, so something has to be discarded. What you discard, and when you decide, is the most consequential observability choice you will make — and unlike most of them, it cannot be revisited after the incident.

    
Spring Boot's default is management.tracing.sampling.probability=0.1: ten per cent, decided at the head of the trace, before anything is known about how it went. Note that this differs from the OpenTelemetry specification's own default of always-on, so the number is Spring's judgement about not overwhelming your backend rather than a standard.

Head versus tail, precisely

    
Head sampling decides at the first span, before the request has done anything. It is cheap — the decision propagates in the traceparent flags, so downstream services do no work for an unsampled trace — and it is blind: a request that turns out to be slow or to fail has exactly the same chance of being kept as one that was fine.

    
Tail sampling buffers the complete trace and decides afterwards, which allows the only rule anybody actually wants: keep everything interesting. Keep all traces with an error. Keep all traces slower than a threshold. Keep a small percentage of the healthy remainder, for a baseline. The cost is that it happens in the collector rather than the application, needs memory to buffer traces in flight, and requires all spans of a trace to reach the same collector instance — which is a real deployment constraint, usually solved with a load-balancing exporter in front of a collector tier.

    
In OpenTelemetry the samplers are AlwaysOn, AlwaysOff, TraceIdRatioBased and ParentBased, and the last is the important one: it makes a child span follow its parent's decision, which is what keeps a trace whole rather than half-sampled. Tail sampling is not a sampler at all — it is the collector's tail_sampling processor, in the contrib distribution, with policies for latency, status code, attributes and rate limits that you compose.


    

      
What I would actually run

      
Tail sampling, with: keep 100% of traces containing an error, 100% slower than your p99 objective, and 1–5% of everything else. It costs a collector tier and it is the difference between “we cannot find that request” and “here it is.” If tail sampling is out of reach, run head sampling at a rate you can afford and accept explicitly that specific-user incidents will often be unanswerable from traces — then make sure your logs are complete and correlated, because they become the fallback. What you should not do is run 10% head sampling, never think about it again, and be surprised in module 01. — An architectural recommendation; the mechanisms are documented, the choice between them is judgement.

    


    
What is not sampled

    
Metrics are aggregates and are not sampled — every request contributes to the counters and histograms regardless of whether its trace was kept. This is why metrics answer “was it slow for everyone” even when traces cannot answer “was it slow for them”, and it is the strongest argument for having both. Logs are usually kept in full for a shorter retention, or sampled by level. A common and defensible arrangement: metrics for thirteen months, traces for a week, logs for thirty days, with sampled traces retained longer if they contained an error.

What OpenTelemetry adds to a log record

    
The OTel log data model is stable and worth knowing even if you never emit OTLP logs, because it is a good checklist of what a log record should contain: Timestamp and ObservedTimestamp (when it happened, and when the collector saw it — they differ, and the difference matters during an outage), SeverityText and a normalised SeverityNumber, a Body that may itself be structured, Attributes, Resource describing the emitting service, EventName, and — the part that matters most here — TraceId, SpanId and TraceFlags.

    
Those last three are what make a log record a member of a trace rather than a standalone line, and they are the reason “show me every log for this request, across every service” is a query rather than a project.


    
Levels, decided once

    
    
      
  • ERROR — something failed that a human should look at, and the request could not be completed. If nobody will act on it, it is not an error. A validation failure that returns 422 to the user is not an error; the system worked.
    • 
      
  • WARN — something recovered, degraded or is heading somewhere bad: a retry succeeded, a cache was unavailable, a deprecated endpoint was called.
    • 
      
  • INFO — the events that describe what the system did, at business granularity. An application was submitted, a review was recorded, a batch completed. These are the lines you will actually read.
    • 
      
  • DEBUG — for a developer, in a development environment, and off in production. If you find yourself wanting DEBUG in production, what you actually want is a span attribute.
    • 
    


    
What must never be logged

    
Stage 9 established the categories and logging is where they leak, because logging is the one place where the whole object gets written. No passwords, no tokens or authorization headers, no full card numbers, no government identifiers, and no whole request bodies from a form that collects any of them. Two specific traps: toString() on an entity that Lombok generated for you will happily print every field including the ones you meant to protect; and a global exception handler that logs the request body for diagnostics will log the login request too. The fix is boring and effective — explicit field lists in log statements, a @ToString.Exclude discipline on sensitive fields, and a redaction filter as a backstop rather than as the primary control.

    
Logs also tend to have the loosest access controls and the longest retention of any telemetry, which makes a leak there disproportionately expensive. Treat the log store as a system holding personal data, because it is.


    
Cost, briefly

    
Logs are usually the largest observability bill, and the reason is almost always one high-volume INFO line in a hot path. Before adding a line inside a loop or a per-request handler, multiply by your request rate: a single 200-byte line at a thousand requests a second is seventeen gigabytes a day. Sampling logs is legitimate for high-volume informational events — keep every error, keep a fraction of the routine ones — and is much better than the usual alternative, which is turning the level up and losing the errors too.

08 / OBJECTIVES
How reliable is reliable enough, written as a number

  
“The API should be fast and available” cannot be alerted on, cannot be traded against feature work, and cannot be violated. Which makes it useless, however much everyone agrees with it.


  

    
An SLI is an indicator: a measurable property of the service, best expressed as a ratio of good events to total events. An SLO is a target for that indicator over a window — 99.9% of requests under 500 ms over 28 days. An SLA is a contract with consequences, and you should not have one that is tighter than your SLO.

    
What the SLO buys you is the error budget: 100% minus the objective, expressed in requests you are permitted to fail. It converts reliability from an argument into arithmetic — if the budget is intact you can ship; if it is exhausted you fix reliability first. Nobody has to win a debate.

Why burn rate rather than a threshold

    
The obvious alert is “page me when the error rate exceeds 1%”, and it is wrong in both directions. It fires on a thirty-second blip that consumed a negligible part of the budget, and it stays silent through a week of 0.9% errors that consumed all of it. Burn rate fixes both by asking a different question: how fast, relative to the objective, is the budget being consumed? A burn rate of 1 exhausts the budget exactly at the end of the window. A burn rate of 14.4 exhausts it in about two days.

    
The multi-window arrangement in the alert list above is from the SRE workbook, and each part earns its place. The long window decides significance — 14.4× sustained for an hour has burned 2% of a 30-day budget, which is worth waking someone for. The short window, one twelfth of the long one, decides whether it is still happening, so that an alert resolves promptly after the incident ends instead of hours later. And the slow burn — 1× over three days — catches the chronic degradation that no threshold alert ever notices.


    
Choosing the objective, which is not an engineering decision

    
The number should come from what users need and what the business will pay for, not from what the service currently achieves. Two practical anchors. First, an SLO tighter than your dependencies can support is fiction: if your database is 99.9% available, your service is not going to be 99.99%. Second, the cost of each nine is roughly an order of magnitude, and the value usually is not — for an internal reviewer dashboard, 99.5% is very likely correct and 99.99% is a way of spending a year of engineering on something nobody asked for.

    
My recommendation for the capstone: one availability SLO and one latency SLO, on the three journeys Stage 10 chose for end-to-end tests — apply, review, award. Three objectives, reviewed quarterly, is a real practice. Thirty is a spreadsheet.


    
Alert fatigue is a measurable failure

    
If more than a small fraction of pages are actionable, the system is training people to ignore it, and that training generalises to the page that mattered. The measures worth tracking are unglamorous and effective: what fraction of pages led to an action, how many fired outside business hours, and how many were for the same cause. An alert that has fired six times without anyone doing anything should be deleted or fixed — leaving it is a decision to have a worse on-call rotation, made by default.


    

      
Challenge

      
Write one SLO for the capstone's application-submission endpoint, as a single sentence containing a ratio, a threshold and a window. Then write the two Prometheus recording rules it needs — the good-event count and the total count — and the burn-rate alert expression for the 1-hour/5-minute window. It is about fifteen lines of YAML, and producing it is the difference between having read about SLOs and having one.

The capstone increment

    
    
      
  • Structured logs everywhere — logging.structured.format.console=ecs, correlation IDs on every line, business events at INFO with explicit field lists, and a review of every log statement that writes an object wholesale.
    • 
      
  • One trace from the browser to the INSERT — OpenTelemetry in the React app propagating traceparent, the API allowing that header through CORS, spring-boot-starter-opentelemetry on the service, spans for the submission and the eligibility evaluation, and the database call visible as a child span.
    • 
      
  • Business metrics through the Observation API — applications submitted, reviews completed, awards issued, with low-cardinality tags you would put on a dashboard and high-cardinality ones you would search a trace by. Plus the HighCardinalityTagsDetector switched on in staging.
    • 
      
  • Actuator exposed deliberately — a separate management port, health, info and prometheus only, health details only when authorised, and liveness and readiness probes that mean different things.
    • 
      
  • Percentile histograms, not client-side percentiles, with explicit SLO buckets matching the objectives below.
    • 
      
  • A dashboard per journey, not per server: rate, errors and duration for apply, review and award, with the p50/p95/p99 from aggregated buckets — and USE panels for the connection pool, thread pool and heap underneath, for after the page.
    • 
      
  • Tail sampling in the collector — every error, everything past the latency objective, and a few per cent of the rest.
    • 
      
  • Two SLOs and their burn-rate alerts — one availability, one latency, on the submission journey, with the 14.4×/1h, 6×/6h and 1×/3d windows wired up and pointed at somewhere a person will actually see them.
    • 
      
  • One deliberate incident — break something in a staging environment, then answer all seven of module 01's questions using only your telemetry. Whatever you could not answer is the next piece of work, and this exercise is worth more than any amount of dashboard building.

Where your deck helps, and where it stops

    
Slides 219–224 on Actuator are genuinely useful: the dependency, the exposure configuration, the endpoint table, /health and /info, and — creditably — a note about securing it in production, which many treatments skip. Slides 243–248 on SLF4J and Logback are correct on levels, per-package configuration and file output, and the mention of JSON logs for ELK points in the right direction. Read both sets.

    
What is absent is everything that turns those into an answer for a user: no Micrometer beyond the endpoint existing, no Prometheus, no Grafana, no alerting, no distributed tracing or correlation IDs, no SLOs or error budgets, and no on-call perspective at all. The deck teaches you to expose /actuator/metrics and never asks which metric you would look at, at 3am, with a ticket that says it was slow at 14:32.
