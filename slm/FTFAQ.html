If you're new to the field, read sections 1–2 in order; everything else can be browsed by curiosity.

1. Foundations

Large Language Model (LLM)

A neural network — usually a stack of Transformer layers — trained on enormous amounts of text to do one job: predict the next token given the tokens before it.

Because the training corpus contains everything from code to literature to dialogue, the network builds rich internal representations of grammar, facts, and reasoning patterns as a side-effect of getting good at its prediction job. Modern LLMs have billions of parameters; the largest have hundreds of billions.

All of fine-tuning exists to take this raw next-token predictor and turn it into something more useful — an assistant, a reasoner, a domain expert.

DataCamp introTuring — LLM trends

Small Language Model (SLM)

The same architecture as an LLM, just with far fewer parameters (typically < 7 B). Built so they can run on a laptop, phone, or single GPU.

SLMs trade raw breadth of knowledge for speed, cost, and privacy. Fine-tuning is especially impactful for SLMs because the gap between their default capabilities and a specialist's can be closed with a few hours of training.

Most production systems do not need a 400 B-parameter model — a well-fine-tuned 1–8 B SLM is often cheaper, faster, and good enough.

Unsloth fine-tuning guideSingle-GPU LoRA guide

Token

A small chunk of text — often a word or piece of a word — that the model treats as one atomic unit of input or output. "Tokenization" is what slices a sentence into tokens.

Models do not see characters or letters directly; they see integer IDs that index into a vocabulary of tokens. A 1 000-word document might be ~1 300 tokens. Cost, context length, and throughput are all measured in tokens.

If a model has a "128 k context length", it can attend to roughly 128 000 tokens at once — about 100 000 words.

Tokenizer

The bidirectional translator between text strings and the token IDs the model actually consumes.

Hugging Face's AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(...) loads the exact tokenizer used during pre-training. Mismatching tokenizers between training and inference is one of the most common silent bugs in fine-tuning.

HF training doc

Weights / Parameters

The numbers inside the network that the training process changes. Everything the model "knows" is encoded in these numbers.

A 7 B-parameter model has 7 × 10⁹ floating-point numbers organised into many matrices (attention projections, MLPs, embeddings, etc.). Fine-tuning either updates all of them (full FT) or a tiny fraction of them (PEFT).

Pre-training

The first, expensive training stage where the model learns to predict the next token from a vast, generic corpus. Done once by labs with GPU clusters.

Pre-training is what turns random initialised weights into a model with general linguistic and world knowledge. Fine-tuning never starts from random weights — it always starts from a pre-trained checkpoint.

Fine-Tuning

Continuing training of a pre-trained model on a smaller, task-specific dataset so it specialises in something the base model was not optimised for.

Hugging Face's training guide states it directly: fine-tuning "continues training a large pre-trained model on a smaller dataset specific to a task or domain" and requires far less compute, data, and time than training from scratch.

Fine-tuning is valuable when (a) data is scarce, (b) the domain has specialised jargon, or (c) accuracy demands exceed what the base model alone can deliver.

HF training docFirst-LLM walkthrough

Transfer Learning

The general idea that knowledge learned on one task can be reused on another. Fine-tuning is the modern, dominant form of transfer learning in NLP.

Zero-shot

Asking a model to perform a task it was never explicitly trained for, with no labelled examples — purely from its general pre-training and the natural-language instruction in the prompt.

When zero-shot performance is good enough, you may not need to fine-tune at all. When it is not, fine-tuning is the next lever.

2. Training Mechanics

Loss Function

A single number that says how wrong the model's current prediction was. Training is just the process of pushing this number down.

For language models, the standard loss is cross-entropy on the next token. For classification fine-tunes, it is cross-entropy over class labels.

Gradient & Backpropagation

A gradient is a vector pointing in the direction that increases the loss the most. Subtract a small piece of it from the weights and the loss goes down. Backpropagation is the calculus trick that computes this gradient efficiently for every parameter.

Optimizer

The algorithm that decides exactly how to take a gradient step — how much to move, with what momentum, how to combine past steps.

Optimizer choice has a much bigger effect on training speed and stability than people expect. AdamW has been default for years; Muon and NorMuon are 2025 alternatives that converge faster.

AdamW

An optimizer that combines momentum (smoothed past gradients) with per-parameter learning rates (each weight gets its own step size based on its own history) and decoupled weight decay (a clean form of regularisation).

Default for most LLM fine-tuning. The "W" stands for "weight decay decoupled".

Learning Rate (LR)

The size of each step the optimizer takes. Too high → training diverges; too low → it crawls or gets stuck.

Full fine-tuning typically uses 1e-5 to 1e-6. LoRA and other PEFT methods use much higher learning rates — 1e-4 to 1e-3 — because only a small subspace is being updated. The Hugging Face PEFT guide notes LoRA "often uses a higher LR than full fine-tuning."

TRL PEFT integration

Epoch / Step / Batch

A batch is a group of examples processed together. A step is one gradient update from one batch. An epoch is one full pass through your training data.

Most fine-tuning runs use 1–3 epochs. Many epochs on a small dataset is the fastest route to overfitting.

Gradient Accumulation

A trick to simulate a large batch on small hardware. Instead of stepping after every batch, you accumulate gradients across N small batches and step once.

A per-device batch of 2 with gradient accumulation 8 behaves (for the optimizer) like a batch of 16, but uses only batch-of-2 worth of memory at any moment. Standard recipe for fitting 7 B+ models on a single GPU.

Learning-Rate Schedule

A rule for how the learning rate changes over training — often a short warmup followed by a slow decay.

Cosine decay with linear warmup is the most common default. Schedules matter most when training is long; for short fine-tunes the difference is small.

Weight Decay

A penalty that gently pulls weights toward zero, discouraging the model from relying on huge magnitudes that tend to overfit.

Overfitting

When the model gets very good on its training data but worse on data it has never seen — it has memorised rather than generalised.

Causes include too many epochs, too small a dataset, too high a rank, too many duplicated examples, and lack of regularisation. Watching validation loss is the canonical defence.

Early Stopping

Halting training the moment validation loss stops improving, even if epochs remain.

3. The Theory of Adaptation

Intrinsic Dimension

The smallest number of independent directions that a model needs to be free to move in to solve a new task — even if it has billions of total parameters.

Empirically, this number is shockingly small: RoBERTa-Large (355 M params) can be adapted in a subspace of about 200 dimensions while retaining over 90 % of full-fine-tuning performance.

This is the mathematical reason PEFT works at all. If the meaningful changes live in a small subspace, updating the full parameter set is wasteful and even harmful.

LoRA & QLoRA 2026 guideDaily Dose of DS

Low-Rank Hypothesis

The idea that the change a fine-tuning step needs to make to a weight matrix can be expressed as a product of two skinny matrices — i.e., it has low rank.

If true, you can store the change as ΔW = B·A with rank r ≪ original, and update only B and A. This is the foundation of LoRA.

arXiv:2106.09685 — LoRA

Pre-trained Manifold

A way of saying that the pre-trained model's weights sit on a curved surface in parameter space where reasoning works. Fine-tuning that drags weights too far off this surface destroys general capability.

This intuition is why we worry about catastrophic forgetting and why low-rank methods that stay close to the original weights are valuable.

Catastrophic Forgetting

The phenomenon where a model, while learning a new task, loses its ability to do tasks it previously could.

A "weight shift" problem: the optimizer minimises the new objective, but the resulting weights are far from the minima of the old (pre-training) objective. Empirical study: SOAP — Mitigating Catastrophic Forgetting in Fine-Tuned LLMs.

This is why PEFT (which moves weights only a little) and data mixing (which keeps training on some original data) are useful defences.

Stability vs. Plasticity

The fundamental tension in fine-tuning: you want the model to be plastic enough to learn the new task, but stable enough to keep what it already knew.

Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC)

A regularisation technique that penalises changes to parameters the model considered important during pre-training, slowing how quickly those weights drift.

A counter-measure against catastrophic forgetting, often used in continual-learning settings.

Data Mixing

Sprinkling a small percentage (typically 5–10 %) of the original pre-training data into the fine-tuning dataset so the model never fully forgets its general knowledge.

Over-specialization

When a fine-tuned model becomes too good at the narrow distribution of the fine-tuning set and worse on out-of-distribution prompts.

Model Staleness

Operational term: the rapid evolution of base models means a fine-tune you ship today is often obsolete a few months later. You will be re-doing this work.

4. The PEFT Family

PEFT — Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning

An umbrella term for any technique that adapts a large pre-trained model by training only a small fraction of its parameters while keeping the rest frozen.

Motivated by intrinsic-dimensionality results and by sheer practicality — adapting a 70 B model in full is unaffordable for most teams. Methods include adapters, prefix-tuning, P-tuning, LoRA, QLoRA, DoRA, and many more.

arXiv:2410.19878 — PEFT Surveyhuggingface/peftTRL PEFT integration

Adapter aka adapter modules

Small extra feed-forward networks inserted between transformer layers. The original transformer is frozen; only the adapters train.

Different adapters can be swapped at inference time to give the same base model multiple personalities — useful for multi-task or continual-learning setups.

Prefix-Tuning

Instead of changing the model's weights, prepend a few trainable vectors to the keys and values of every attention layer. Tuning these "soft prefixes" steers attention without touching the model.

P-Tuning v2

Prefix-tuning generalised: a deep prompt module that adds learnable prompt tokens at every layer, with a reparameterisation that makes them stable to train.

Surprisingly competitive with full fine-tuning on many NLU tasks while training a tiny number of parameters.

Prompt Tuning

The extreme: only add a few trainable embedding vectors at the input. Cheapest of the PEFT family; works well at scale but can be brittle on small models.

LoRA — Low-Rank Adaptation

Freeze every weight matrix W₀ in the model. Express the update ΔW as the product of two skinny matrices B·A (rank r). Train only A and B.

h = W₀x + B·A·x     with r ≪ min(d, k)

B is initialised to zero and A to a Gaussian, so at the start ΔW = 0 — the fine-tuned model behaves identically to the base. The LoRA paper reports a 10 000× reduction in trainable parameters and a 3× drop in GPU memory while matching full fine-tuning.

LoRA has near-zero inference overhead because B·A can be merged back into W₀ after training, leaving the original architecture unchanged.

arXiv:2106.09685 — LoRAhuggingface/peft

Rank (r)

The "skinniness" of the LoRA matrices A and B — the inner dimension of their product. Bigger r = more expressive update, more parameters, more risk of overfitting.

Heuristics: r = 8 for a style change; r = 16 as a sane default; r = 64 or higher for reasoning-heavy adaptation.

LoRA α (alpha)

A scalar that scales the LoRA update before adding it to the frozen weights. Effective scale = α / r.

α decouples "how big the update is" from "what rank you chose," letting you change r without massively retuning the learning rate.

LoRA Dropout

Standard dropout applied inside the LoRA path during training to reduce overfitting on the small adapter parameters.

QLoRA — Quantized LoRA

Run LoRA on top of a frozen base model that has been compressed to 4-bit precision. Gradients flow through the quantized base into the adapters; the base itself is never updated.

The QLoRA paper shows that 65 B-parameter models can be fine-tuned on a single 48 GB GPU with performance comparable to 16-bit fine-tuning. Three innovations: NF4, Double Quantization, Paged Optimizers.

arXiv:2305.14314 — QLoRA

NF4 — NormalFloat-4

A 4-bit number format whose representable values are spaced according to the bell-shaped distribution that neural-network weights actually follow. This wastes fewer bits than uniform 4-bit integers.

Double Quantization

Quantization usually keeps small "scaling constants" in higher precision. Double quantization quantizes those constants too, saving a bit more memory.

Paged Optimizers

A trick that uses NVIDIA's unified memory to page optimizer state out to CPU RAM during memory spikes, preventing out-of-memory crashes on long sequences.

DoRA — Weight-Decomposed Low-Rank Adaptation

Decompose every weight vector into two parts: a magnitude (how big it is) and a direction (where it points). Update direction with LoRA; let magnitude move freely.

Empirically, full fine-tuning lets magnitude and direction vary independently; LoRA forces them to move proportionally. DoRA fixes this and matches full fine-tuning on commonsense reasoning and visual instruction tuning.

arXiv:2402.09353 — DoRADoRA explainedJHU lecture deckr/LocalLLaMA discussion

EDoRA — Efficient DoRA

DoRA made cheaper by initialising adapters via Singular Value Decomposition rather than random matrices.

arXiv:2501.12067 — EDoRA

QA-LoRA / LQ-LoRA / IntLoRA

Variants of QLoRA that experiment with different ways of combining low-rank adaptation with quantized weights — different number formats, different placement of quantization, different gradient flow.

bitsandbytes

The widely used Python library that provides 4-bit and 8-bit quantization kernels and paged optimizers behind the scenes for QLoRA.

5. Alignment & Reinforcement Learning

SFT — Supervised Fine-Tuning

Standard, supervised next-token training on a curated dataset of (instruction, response) pairs. Teaches the model the format and style of an assistant.

Almost always the first stage of any alignment pipeline. The output is sometimes called an "SFT model" or "policy".

Instruction Tuning

A specific flavour of SFT focused on teaching the model to follow natural-language instructions across many tasks.

ChatML / Jinja Templates

Standardised text formats with special tokens marking system, user, and assistant turns. The tokenizer turns these into the structured token sequences the model expects.

Mismatched templates between training and inference is a classic source of bugs — the model behaves "weirdly" because it never sees the format it was trained on.

LIMA Hypothesis

"Less Is More for Alignment": as few as ~1 000 highly diverse, high-quality examples can be enough for SFT. Quality > quantity.

RLHF — Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback

A three-stage pipeline: (1) start with an SFT model, (2) train a reward model from human preference rankings, (3) use reinforcement learning (usually PPO) to update the language model so it produces high-reward outputs.

RLHF lets models learn from human preferences rather than from hand-engineered metrics. It is unstable and expensive — you must keep four models in memory simultaneously — and prone to reward hacking.

HF RLHF blogIonio comprehensive guidearXiv:2507.19672 — Alignment & safety survey

Reward Model (RM)

A separate neural network trained on pairs of "chosen vs. rejected" outputs labelled by humans. It learns to score new outputs the way the labellers would.

The RM becomes the source of training signal for the policy in PPO-RLHF. It is also the part of the pipeline most vulnerable to bias and reward hacking.

PPO — Proximal Policy Optimization

A reinforcement-learning algorithm that updates a policy cautiously — each step is constrained so it cannot move too far from the previous policy. This caution is what the "Proximal" in the name refers to.

In LLM RLHF, PPO uses four models simultaneously: actor (current policy), critic (value model), reference model, and reward model. Memory-intensive but stable.

KL-Divergence Penalty

A measure of how much one probability distribution differs from another. In RLHF, a KL penalty between the new policy and the original (reference) model is added to the reward, preventing the model from drifting linguistically into nonsense.

Reward Hacking

When a model finds a strategy that gets a high reward score without actually doing what humans wanted — "gaming" the reward model.

Classic examples: producing flattery-laden but factually empty text, or exploiting tokens the reward model over-weights.

Policy / Actor / Critic / Reference Model

Roles in PPO. Policy = the LM you are training. Actor = same as policy, viewed as the action-chooser. Critic = a value model estimating how good a state is. Reference model = a frozen copy of the SFT model used to compute the KL penalty.

DPO — Direct Preference Optimization

Skip the reward model entirely. Reformulate the RLHF objective so you can directly maximise the probability of chosen responses and minimise the probability of rejected responses, all with a simple classification-style loss.

DPO matches or surpasses PPO-RLHF on tasks like sentiment control and summarisation while being far simpler — no reward model, no sampling during training, fewer hyperparameters.

arXiv:2305.18290 — DPO

GRPO — Group Relative Policy Optimization

A reinforcement-learning algorithm where, for each query, you generate a group of G sample answers and compute each one's advantage relative to the group's mean and standard deviation.

Ai = ( ri − mean(r1…G) ) / std(r1…G)

Eliminates the value model that PPO needs (huge memory savings) and gives a cleaner relative signal that empirically works very well for reasoning. The key algorithm behind DeepSeek-R1.

Math behind GRPOWhy GRPO mattersA critical rebuttal

Group-Relative Advantage

Instead of asking "how good was this answer?" in absolute terms, GRPO asks "how good was this answer compared to its siblings drawn from the same prompt?"

This is statistically a low-variance estimator of advantage and avoids the need for a separate critic.

Chain-of-Thought (CoT)

Letting the model write out its intermediate reasoning steps before producing a final answer — usually with a prompt like "Let's think step by step."

Long-CoT traces are the cold-start data behind reasoning-tuned models like DeepSeek-R1.

Cold Start (DeepSeek-R1)

A small initial round of SFT on a few thousand high-quality long-CoT examples, just enough to give the model a reasoning template before reinforcement learning takes over.

arXiv:2501.12948v1

Rejection Sampling

Generate many candidate answers, keep only the correct ones (verified by reward / regex / unit tests), and use them as new training data.

In the DeepSeek-R1 pipeline, ~800 000 reasoning samples were generated and filtered before a second SFT round.

RLAIF — RL from AI Feedback

RLHF where the "human labellers" are replaced by another LLM. Cheaper, faster, scalable — but inherits the labeller LLM's biases.

6. Numeric Precision & Memory

FP32 / FP16 / BF16

Different ways of storing floating-point numbers. FP32 = 32 bits per number, FP16 = 16 bits, BF16 = 16 bits but with a wider exponent (more dynamic range, less precision).

BF16 is the standard for modern LLM training because it tolerates the wide value ranges that appear in deep networks. FP16 needs loss scaling tricks; BF16 typically does not.

Quantization

Compressing weights from 16-bit or 32-bit floats down to 8-bit or 4-bit integers (or specialised formats like NF4) to save memory.

Quantization is what makes a 65 B model fit on a single 48 GB GPU in QLoRA. The art is doing it without losing meaningful accuracy.

DeepSpeed ZeRO (Stages 1, 2, 3)

A family of memory-saving strategies for distributed training. Stage 1 shards optimizer state across GPUs; Stage 2 also shards gradients; Stage 3 also shards parameters.

Higher stage = less memory per GPU but more communication. ZeRO-3 lets you train a model larger than any single GPU can hold.

microsoft/deepspeedDeepSpeed × Hugging Face PEFT

FSDP — Fully Sharded Data Parallel

PyTorch's native equivalent of ZeRO-3 — shards parameters, gradients, and optimizer state across GPUs and gathers them just-in-time during forward / backward.

CPU / Parameter Offload

When GPU memory is tight, push some of the data — optimizer states, gradients, or even parameters — out to CPU RAM and bring it back only when needed.

Slower than keeping everything on GPU, but often the only way to fit very large models on small hardware.

Gradient Checkpointing (Activation Checkpointing)

During the forward pass, throw away most intermediate activations. During the backward pass, recompute them on demand. Trades some compute for a big drop in memory usage.

FlashAttention

A custom CUDA kernel that computes attention without materialising the giant intermediate matrices, making attention much faster and less memory-hungry — especially at long context lengths.

7. Modern Optimizers

Muon

An optimizer (introduced 2025) that orthogonalises the matrix-shaped weight updates so each layer's update becomes a clean rotation rather than a skewed stretch.

Empirically reaches the same loss as AdamW in roughly half the training steps on LLM workloads.

arXiv:2502.16982 — Muon Scalability

NorMuon — Neuron-Wise Normalised Muon

Muon, but with a row-wise normalisation step after orthogonalisation so no individual neuron's weights dominate. Adds another ~11 % training-efficiency gain over plain Muon.

arXiv:2510.05491 — NorMuon

A Python class that wraps the standard training loop — forward, backward, optimizer step, evaluation, checkpointing — so you usually don't need to write any of it yourself.

9. Data & Evaluation

Instruction Dataset

A collection of (instruction, response) pairs used for SFT. Public examples include Alpaca, ShareGPT, and Capybara on the Hugging Face Datasets Hub.

Preference Dataset

A collection of (prompt, chosen response, rejected response) triples. Used to train a reward model (for RLHF) or directly with DPO. UltraFeedback is a well-known example.

MinHash Deduplication

A probabilistic algorithm that quickly estimates how similar two text documents are by hashing their shingles. Used to remove near-duplicate examples that cause overfitting.

LLM-as-a-Judge

Using a strong LLM (often GPT-4o) to score data quality or rank model outputs — replacing or augmenting human labellers.

Cheap and fast; requires careful prompt design to avoid systematic biases (e.g., the judge preferring its own style).

MMLU

"Massive Multitask Language Understanding" — a 57-subject multiple-choice benchmark spanning everything from US history to professional medicine. Long the standard general-knowledge yardstick.

LLM benchmarks compared

GSM8K

A grade-school math word-problem benchmark that requires multi-step arithmetic reasoning.

HumanEval

A 164-problem Python coding benchmark where the model must write a function that passes given unit tests.

GPQA Diamond

PhD-level science multiple-choice questions designed to be Google-proof — useful for distinguishing top reasoning models that have saturated MMLU.

SWE-bench

A benchmark of real GitHub issues that the model must resolve by navigating large codebases and writing functional fixes. As of 2025, even Claude 3.5 Sonnet only solves ~26 %.

Benchmark Saturation

When models reach scores near 100 % on a benchmark, making it impossible to differentiate between them. The benchmark is no longer informative.

Deepchecks — Top benchmarkshackerllama — LLM evalsLaunchDarkly — LLM evaluation

Data Contamination

When test-set questions have leaked into the training corpus. A model can then "remember" answers rather than reason them out, inflating reported scores.

A 2023 study found GSM8K accuracy dropped by up to 13 % on de-contaminated variants — "much of the score was memorisation."

Chatbot Arena (LMSYS)

A crowdsourced benchmark where users blind-rank pairs of model outputs to the same prompt, producing an Elo-style leaderboard reflecting human preference.

10. Reasoning & Chain-of-Thought

Reasoning Fine-Tuning

A specialised flavour of fine-tuning whose goal is not just to change the model's style or domain, but to teach it to think out loud — generate intermediate steps before producing an answer.

Distinct from generic SFT in four ways: (1) the model must produce a reasoning trace, (2) that trace must actually lead to the final answer (faithfulness), (3) high-quality reasoning data is hard to obtain, and (4) reasoning often demands long contexts.

"Putting on the Thinking Hats" survey (arXiv:2510.13170)

Reflex Model vs. Thinking Model

A Reflex Model answers instinctively from the prompt; a Thinking Model deliberates step by step before answering. Reasoning fine-tuning is the bridge between them — typically achieved via SFT on CoT data followed by reinforced fine-tuning.

Six Thinking Hats Framework

A taxonomy borrowed from Edward de Bono and applied by Chen et al. (2026) to organise CoT fine-tuning into six dimensions: detailed planning (blue), divergent thinking (green), intuitive judgment (red), timely reflection (black), internal reasoning (yellow), and fact perception (white).

Useful for diagnosing what kind of reasoning a model lacks — e.g., a model strong on planning but weak on reflection benefits from different fine-tuning data than one weak on fact perception.

Chain-of-Thought (CoT) — in depth

A sequence of intermediate reasoning steps produced by the model on its way to a final answer. Originally a prompting trick ("Let's think step by step"), now a training target.

Two regimes: (1) zero-shot CoT prompting at inference time, (2) CoT fine-tuning where the model is trained on (prompt, reasoning, answer) triples so the behaviour becomes default. Reasoning RL (GRPO + verifiable rewards) further sharpens it.

IBM — What is CoT prompting

Pre-thinking SFT

The dominant paradigm: train the model to output the reasoning first and the answer last. Cross-entropy is computed over the whole concatenation (question + reasoning + answer).

Enables genuine problem decomposition. Vulnerable to unfaithfulness and error accumulation.

Post-thinking SFT

The model emits the answer first, then explains. Faster, less error-accumulation-prone, but loses problem-decomposition ability — best for small models on simple tasks.

Multi-task SFT (with prefix tokens)

Use a special token at the start of input to tell the model whether to produce reasoning or only the answer. One model, two modes.

Improves training stability and inference speed but, by separating reasoning from answer generation, can increase unfaithfulness if not handled carefully.

CoT Faithfulness

Whether the reasoning trace actually causes and justifies the final answer, rather than being a plausible-looking story bolted on after the fact.

A NAACL 2025 study found that ordinary RLHF, SFT, and QLoRA fine-tuning reduce CoT faithfulness on average, even when they improve task accuracy. Measuring faithfulness is an open research problem; common approaches include perturbation tests and LLM-as-judge verification.

arXiv:2411.15382 — Impact of Fine-Tuning on CoT

Error Accumulation

A wrong reasoning step early in the chain corrupts everything that follows. Pre-thinking SFT is especially exposed to this.

DCoT-style multi-chain training mitigates this by letting the model compare divergent paths and self-correct.

Manual Annotation of Reasoning

Humans write the reasoning traces. Two flavours: implicit (annotators highlight supporting spans, e.g., e-SNLI) and explicit (annotators write step-by-step reasoning, e.g., GSM8K, MATH).

High-fidelity but expensive and domain-specific.

Automatic Annotation of Reasoning

Use a strong reasoning LLM (e.g., DeepSeek-R1, GPT-4) to generate reasoning traces for a smaller student model. Or convert structured data (knowledge graphs) into reasoning paths via rules.

Cheap and scalable — but requires an answer verifier to filter incorrect traces before training.

Answer Verifier (Reasoning Filter)

A check that decides whether a generated reasoning trace landed on the correct answer. For math/code, this can be exact match or a unit test; for open-domain tasks, it can be an LLM judge.

The reasoning vs. content Fields

In Together AI's reasoning-fine-tuning data format, every assistant turn splits into a reasoning field (the chain of thought) and a content field (the final answer). Training a reasoning model without this split — or with the reasoning collapsed into content — can degrade the model's reasoning ability.

RFT — Reinforced Fine-Tuning

A second post-SFT stage that uses a reward signal to push the model toward better reasoning. Umbrella for PPO/RLHF, GRPO/DAPO/GSPO, DPO/CPO, and STaR-style self-training.

Process Reward vs. Outcome Reward

Outcome reward scores only the final answer (1 if correct, 0 otherwise). Process reward scores each step of the reasoning trace, assigning credit step-by-step.

Outcome rewards are cheap to compute (especially for math via exact match) but blind to bad reasoning paths that happen to land on the right answer. Process rewards align supervision with the reasoning itself but require expensive step-level annotation or a trained process-reward model.

GenRM-CoT (Generative Reward Model with CoT)

A reward model implemented as a generative LLM that produces a critique or evaluation of a candidate reasoning trace, rather than a single scalar score. Often itself uses chain-of-thought to justify its rating.

Rule-Based Reward

Reward = 1 if the model's final answer matches the reference, 0 otherwise. No trained reward model needed.

Highly effective for math and coding (where verification is mechanical) and was the core reward signal for the GRPO stage of DeepSeek-R1.

DAPO / GSPO / DRA-GRPO

Variants of GRPO that fix specific failure modes: DAPO and GSPO address entropy collapse (the policy becoming too deterministic during RL); DRA-GRPO adds diversity rewards so the model continues to explore different reasoning paths.

Token-Level DPO & CPO

Refinements of DPO. Token-level DPO moves preference supervision from full-sequence to per-token granularity. CPO (Contrastive Preference Optimization) eliminates even the reference model that vanilla DPO needs.

STaR — Self-Taught Reasoner

A self-training loop: have the model generate many reasoning trajectories, keep only the ones that lead to the correct answer, then SFT on those. Bootstraps reasoning ability without external annotators.

The DeepSeek-R1 pipeline's rejection sampling stage is essentially STaR at scale (~800 000 generated traces filtered by correctness).

DCoT — Divergent Chain-of-Thought

During training, ask the model to produce several reasoning chains for the same prompt and then select among them. Tuning on multi-chain data teaches the model to compare its own reasoning paths.

Empirically improves reasoning across model scales (1.3 B – 70 B) — and helps smaller models approach the reasoning quality of much larger ones via self-correction.

Self-Correction

The model's ability to notice and fix its own mistakes mid-reasoning. Emerges from divergent multi-chain training, RL with verifiable rewards, and reflection-style prompts.

Select2Reason

A data-selection framework that picks the most useful 10 % of a long-CoT instruction set by ranking examples on (a) estimated question difficulty and (b) reasoning-trace length.

On OpenR1-Math-220k, fine-tuning on this 10 % matches or beats full-data tuning across nine math benchmarks. Implication: synthetic CoT scale is wasteful — quality + length dominates.

FRODO

A counterfactual-training approach that uses mutual-information objectives to align the final answer with its reasoning chain — directly attacking unfaithfulness.

Distillation (Reasoning Distillation)

Train a small "student" model on the output traces of a large "teacher" model so the student inherits the teacher's behaviour at a fraction of the cost.

DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen3-8B was created by distilling reasoning patterns from the 671 B DeepSeek-R1 into Alibaba's Qwen3-8B base, using ~800 000 high-quality reasoning samples. Despite its size, it is SOTA among open-source 8 B models on AIME 2024.

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE)

Instead of running every token through every parameter, route each token through only a small subset of expert sub-networks. Total parameters can be huge while compute per token stays small.

DeepSeek-V3.2 and Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B use sparse MoE. Qwen3-Next has 512 routing experts + 1 shared expert and activates just 10 per token — 80 B total parameters with only 3 B active per token.

Sparse Attention

An attention pattern where each token attends to only a subset of other tokens (rather than all of them), reducing the quadratic cost of long contexts.

Critical for reasoning models that may emit thousands of CoT tokens. DeepSeek-V3.2 uses sparse attention to support contexts up to ~1 M tokens.

Hybrid Attention

A mix of attention mechanisms in different layers — e.g., Qwen3-Next combines Gated DeltaNet (a linear-attention variant) with Gated Attention — to balance long-range modelling against compute.

Multi-Token Prediction (MTP)

Train the model to predict the next several tokens at once rather than just one. Boosts pre-training quality and unlocks faster speculative-decoding inference.

Long-Context Reasoning

The challenge of reasoning over thousands of tokens — long inputs, long CoTs, or both. Drives the use of sparse attention, MoE, and curriculum learning on increasing context lengths.

AIME / HMMT

AIME (American Invitational Mathematics Examination) and HMMT (Harvard-MIT Mathematics Tournament) are competition math benchmarks now widely used to evaluate reasoning models, since they're harder than GSM8K and resist simple pattern matching.

MATH (dataset)

A 7 500-train / 5 000-test set of competition-level math problems with worked solutions. The standard "hard math" benchmark for CoT fine-tuning.

AQuA

~100 k algebraic word problems with reasoning rationales — a much larger CoT-equipped dataset than GSM8K, useful as pre-training data for math reasoning.

MGSM

A multilingual translation of GSM8K (88 train / 2 750 test) used to evaluate cross-lingual math reasoning.

BBH — BIG-Bench Hard

A 6 511-example test-only benchmark of general reasoning tasks drawn from BIG-Bench that early models struggled with. No CoT is provided in the dataset itself.

MMLU-Pro

A more challenging successor to MMLU with graduate-level Q&A. Partial CoT is provided for some examples; used both for evaluation and as fine-tuning material.

e-SNLI / MultiRC / FEVER

Datasets where reasoning is annotated implicitly — annotators highlight supporting spans rather than write out a chain of thought. Useful as cheaper alternatives to fully written CoT data.

WebGPT & Tool-Use Traces

Reasoning datasets that include not just text reasoning but tool calls — browser actions, calculator use, code execution. Train models that reason while interacting with an environment.

OpenR1-Math-220k

An open synthetic CoT instruction-tuning dataset with ~220 k math examples generated by strong reasoning models. The empirical playground for Select2Reason and similar selection studies.

DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen3-8B

An 8 B reasoning model produced by distilling the 671 B DeepSeek-R1 into Alibaba's Qwen3-8B base using ~800 000 high-quality reasoning samples. MIT-licensed, runs on a single 40–80 GB-VRAM GPU.

Released May 2025; the R1-0528 update jumped its AIME 2025 accuracy from 70 % to 87.5 %. Demonstrates that careful distillation can deliver near-frontier reasoning at small scale.

Qwen3-Next "Thinking"

Alibaba's reasoning-mode variant of Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B. Combines hybrid attention (Gated DeltaNet + Gated Attention), high-sparsity MoE (3 B active out of 80 B), and a 262 144-token native context. ~87.8 % on AIME25 math.
