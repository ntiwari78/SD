Prediction and Entropy of Printed English

Claude E. Shannon (1951)

The paper that connects information theory to modern AI
Section 1

Why Was This Paper Written?

In 1948, Claude Shannon published A Mathematical Theory of Communication, establishing information theory as a formal discipline. That paper gave us the mathematics of information: bits, entropy, channel capacity. But a fundamental question remained unanswered: how much information does English actually contain?

Shannon wanted to quantify three things:

  • How much information does each letter of English carry?
  • How predictable is the English language?
  • How efficiently can English be compressed?

His earlier mathematical models (zero-order, first-order, second-order approximations) gave rough estimates but couldn't capture the full complexity of language — the grammar, idioms, world knowledge, and semantic structure that make English what it is.

In this 1951 paper, Shannon introduced a radically different approach: instead of building mathematical models of language, ask humans to predict the next letter. If humans predict well, English is redundant. If prediction is difficult, English carries more information. This one idea — treating human prediction as a measuring instrument for information — changed the field forever.

The Lake Analogy. Imagine trying to measure how deep a lake is. You could use complex sonar equations, model the lake's geological profile, and simulate wave propagation. Or you could drop a weighted rope and measure directly. Shannon chose the rope. His "rope" was human intuition about language — the vast, implicit statistical knowledge every fluent speaker carries.
Section 2

What is Information?

Shannon defined information not as meaning or content, but as surprise. An event that is expected carries little information. An event that is shocking carries a lot.

"Tomorrow the sun will rise."
No surprise. You already knew this. This statement carries almost zero information.

"India won the FIFA World Cup."
Enormous surprise. This is wildly unexpected. This statement carries a huge amount of information.

Shannon's key insight: Information = Surprise. More surprising events carry more information. Less surprising events carry less.

He formalized this with a beautiful equation. If an event \(x\) has probability \(P(x)\), its information content is:

$$I(x) = -\log_2 P(x) \text{ bits}$$

When \(P(x) = 1\) (certain), \(I(x) = 0\) bits. When \(P(x)\) is very small (rare event), \(I(x)\) is very large. The logarithm base 2 gives us the unit of "bits" — the number of yes/no questions needed to identify the event.

Example. A fair coin: \(P(\text{heads}) = 0.5\). Information: \(-\log_2(0.5) = 1\) bit. One yes/no question resolves the outcome. A fair die: \(P(3) = 1/6\). Information: \(-\log_2(1/6) \approx 2.58\) bits.
Section 3

Entropy

While information measures the surprise of a single event, entropy measures the average surprise across all possible events — the average uncertainty before knowing the next symbol.

$$H = -\sum_{i} P(x_i) \log_2 P(x_i)$$

Entropy answers: "On average, how many bits do I need to describe the outcome of this random process?"

Three Illustrative Examples

1. Fair coin (H = 1 bit). Each outcome is equally likely: \(P(H) = P(T) = 0.5\). Entropy = \(-0.5 \log_2 0.5 - 0.5 \log_2 0.5 = 1\) bit. Maximum uncertainty — you genuinely cannot predict the outcome.

2. Loaded coin (H ≈ 0.08 bits). \(P(H) = 0.99, P(T) = 0.01\). Entropy = \(-0.99\log_2 0.99 - 0.01\log_2 0.01 \approx 0.08\) bits. Almost no uncertainty — you know it will almost certainly be heads.

3. English "TH_" (very low entropy). After "TH", the next letter is almost certainly "E" (or occasionally "A", "I", "O"). The probability distribution is sharply peaked, so entropy is very low. You can predict the next letter with high confidence.

The key pattern: the more predictable a source, the lower its entropy. A completely random source (all outcomes equally likely) has maximum entropy. A perfectly predictable source has zero entropy.

Section 4

Redundancy

If entropy measures the unpredictable part of language, redundancy measures the predictable part — the information that is already implied by context and structure.

English is full of redundancy:

  • Spelling rules: "Q" is almost always followed by "U".
  • Grammar: "The dog ___ the ball" — the missing word is almost certainly a verb.
  • Sentence structure: Subject-verb-object patterns constrain word order.
  • Context: "The weather today is ___" — only a few words make sense.
  • Semantics: "The cat sat on the ___" — you expect "mat," "chair," "floor."

Shannon defined redundancy as the fraction of a message that is predictable:

$$R = 1 - \frac{H}{H_{\max}}$$

Where \(H\) is the actual entropy and \(H_{\max}\) is the maximum possible entropy (if all symbols were equally likely). For English with 27 symbols (26 letters + space), \(H_{\max} = \log_2 27 \approx 4.76\) bits/letter.

Crossword puzzles work because of redundancy. You can fill in missing letters because the surrounding letters heavily constrain the possibilities. If English had zero redundancy, crossword puzzles would be impossible — every letter would be equally likely regardless of context.
Section 5

Shannon's Prediction Game

This is the heart of the paper. Shannon devised a deceptively simple experiment:

  1. Take a passage of English text.
  2. Show it one letter at a time.
  3. Before revealing each letter, ask a human subject to guess what it is.
  4. Record how many guesses it takes to get the right answer.

Some letters are easy:

"THE CAT SAT ON TH_" → People instantly guess "E".

"THE PRESIDENT OF THE UNI_" → You expect "TED" (as in "United States") or "VERSITY".

The key insight: the number of guesses needed reveals the entropy. If a subject always guesses correctly on the first try, entropy is zero — the text is perfectly predictable. If they need many guesses, entropy is high.

Shannon proved that human prediction ability gives both an upper and lower bound on the true entropy of English. The better humans predict, the lower the true entropy must be.

Section 6

Why Humans?

Why not simply compute the statistics of English from a corpus? Why use human subjects at all?

The answer is that humans have internalized an enormous, implicit model of English that no mathematical model of the time could match. Every fluent English speaker carries knowledge of:

  • Letter frequencies: "E" is the most common letter, "Z" the rarest.
  • Bigram and trigram statistics: "TH", "HE", "IN" are common; "QK", "ZX" are not.
  • Spelling rules: "I before E except after C" (mostly).
  • Grammar: Articles precede nouns, verbs follow subjects.
  • Vocabulary: Thousands of words and their usage patterns.
  • World knowledge: "The capital of France is ___" constrains the answer to "Paris."
  • Style and register: Formal vs. informal, scientific vs. literary.

In 1951, no computer could model all of this. Humans were, in effect, the most sophisticated language models available. Shannon recognized this and used humans as measuring instruments rather than trying to build mathematical substitutes.

A prescient framing. By treating humans as language models, Shannon anticipated by 70+ years the core idea behind modern AI: build a system that predicts the next token as well as a human can. GPT is, in a real sense, an artificial Shannon subject.
Section 7

Prediction Improves with Context

One of Shannon's most important findings: the more context you have, the better you can predict, and the lower the entropy.

Know "T" → Many possibilities: TA, TE, TH, TI, TO, TU, TR, ...

Know "TH" → Fewer: THE, THAT, THIS, THEM, THEY, THINK, ...

Know "THE" → Next is very likely a space.

Know "THE PRESIDENT OF" → Prediction becomes much easier — only a handful of completions make sense.

As context length increases, entropy decreases. Shannon showed this experimentally: subjects who could see more preceding text made better predictions.

Section 8

Long-Range Dependencies

Earlier language models only considered nearby characters — the previous 2, 5, or 10 letters. But Shannon recognized that words hundreds of characters earlier can affect the current prediction.

"Although it was raining heavily, John forgot his ___"
The word "raining" at the start of the sentence makes "umbrella" the most likely completion — even though "raining" is many words away from the blank.

"The professor who had studied linguistics in Paris for twenty years finally published her ___"
"Published" suggests a noun like "book" or "paper." The long preceding context about linguistics constrains the topic.

These long-range dependencies pose a fundamental challenge: to capture them, a model must somehow remember and reason about information from far earlier in the text. This is precisely the problem that Transformer attention mechanisms were designed to solve. While Shannon could only use human subjects to capture these dependencies, modern architectures can learn them from data.

Section 9

Markov Models

An \(N\)-order Markov model assumes that the next character depends only on the previous \(N\) characters. Shannon used these as baselines for comparison.

  • Zero-order: All letters equally likely. Random letter soup.
  • First-order: Match English letter frequencies. "E" appears more often than "Z," but no structure.
  • Second-order: Use letter-pair (bigram) statistics. "TH" is common, "QZ" is not.
  • Third-order: Trigram statistics. Starts to look vaguely English-like.

Shannon generated text from each order to illustrate the difference:

# Zero-order (random):   XFOML RXKHRJFFJUJ ZLPWCFWKCYJ
# First-order (freq):    OCRO HLI RGWR NMIELWIS EU LL NBNESEBYA
# Second-order (bigram): ON IE ANTSOUTINYS ARE T INCTORE
# Third-order (trigram): IN NO IST LAT WHEY CRATICT FROURE BIRS

Even third-order Markov models produce gibberish. Natural English depends on context far longer than three characters. Shannon's human prediction experiments showed that the effective "memory" of English extends over hundreds of characters — far beyond any fixed-order Markov model.

Section 10

Conditional Probability

At its core, language prediction is about estimating conditional probabilities:

$$P(\text{next character} \mid \text{previous context})$$

For example:

  • \(P(E \mid TH)\) → Very high (around 0.7)
  • \(P(U \mid Q)\) → Nearly 1.0
  • \(P(\text{space} \mid THE)\) → Very high
  • \(P(Z \mid TH)\) → Nearly 0

The conditional entropy of English is:

$$H(X_n \mid X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_{n-1}) = -\sum P(x_n \mid \text{context}) \log_2 P(x_n \mid \text{context})$$

This is exactly what GPT does. A modern language model takes a sequence of tokens as context and outputs a probability distribution over the next token. The only differences are scale (billions of parameters vs. one human brain) and granularity (tokens vs. characters). The fundamental task — estimate the conditional probability of the next symbol — is identical.

Section 11

The Ideal Predictor

Shannon imagined a hypothetical ideal predictor: one that always assigns the correct probability distribution over next characters. Such a predictor wouldn't always guess right (sometimes the text genuinely goes in an unexpected direction), but its probability estimates would be perfectly calibrated.

Example: "THE CAT SAT ON THE M_"

An ideal predictor might assign:

  • P(A) = 0.45 (MAT, MAN, MAP, ...)
  • P(O) = 0.25 (MOON, MOUSE, ...)
  • P(I) = 0.10 (MILK, MIRROR, ...)
  • P(E) = 0.08 (METAL, ...)
  • Other letters share remaining probability

The closer a predictor's estimates are to the true distribution, the lower the cross-entropy between its predictions and reality — and the lower the estimated information content of the text.

Shannon showed that human subjects approximate this ideal surprisingly well, especially for common patterns. Their main weakness is with rare or unusual constructions — exactly the same weakness that modern language models exhibit.

Section 12

Estimating Entropy Indirectly

Shannon's central methodological innovation was to estimate entropy indirectly through prediction accuracy, rather than computing it directly from probability distributions (which would require impossibly large statistical tables).

The logic is elegant:

  1. Better prediction → Lower uncertainty about the next symbol.
  2. Lower uncertainty → Lower entropy.
  3. Therefore: prediction accuracy is a proxy for entropy.

Specifically, Shannon defined two bounds:

  • Upper bound \(F_N\): Derived from the number of guesses subjects needed.
  • Lower bound \(F_D\): Derived from the probability of guessing correctly on the first try.

The true entropy of English lies somewhere between these bounds. Shannon found that both bounds converged to approximately the same value, giving him confidence in the estimate.

Why this was better. Previous approaches required computing \(P(x_n \mid x_1, \ldots, x_{n-1})\) for every possible context — an astronomically large table. Shannon's method bypassed this entirely by using humans as probability estimators. The approach was not just simpler; it was fundamentally more accurate because humans capture structure that no 1950s mathematical model could represent.

Section 14

Why Compression Works

Shannon's results explain a fact we now take for granted: text compression works remarkably well.

If English had zero redundancy — if every letter were equally unpredictable regardless of context — then compression would be impossible. You cannot compress a truly random sequence. But because English is approximately 75% redundant, compression algorithms can remove the predictable parts and keep only the surprising ones.

This is exactly what modern compression algorithms do:

  • Huffman coding: Assigns shorter codes to more frequent symbols.
  • LZ77/LZ78: Replaces repeated patterns with references to earlier occurrences.
  • Arithmetic coding: Encodes entire messages as single numbers based on cumulative probabilities.
  • Brotli & Zstandard: Modern algorithms combining dictionary matching with entropy coding.

Shannon's entropy provides the theoretical floor: no compression algorithm can compress English text below ~1 bit per character on average. Any algorithm that achieves close to 1 bit/character is approaching Shannon's limit.

In practice: ZIP typically compresses English text to about 2–3 bits/character. More sophisticated algorithms (PPM, CMIX) get closer to 1.5 bits/character. The gap between these numbers and Shannon's 1-bit floor represents structure that current algorithms fail to exploit but that humans intuitively capture.
Section 15

Connection to Modern LLMs

Shannon's 1951 paper described, in miniature, the exact objective that today's large language models optimize. The parallel is uncanny:

The training objective of GPT-style models is literally to minimize cross-entropy loss on next-token prediction — the same quantity Shannon was estimating through his human experiments. Lower cross-entropy means the model's predicted probability distribution is closer to the true distribution of language.

Section 16

Key Takeaways

  • Information is surprise. Predictable events carry little information; surprising events carry a lot.
  • Entropy measures average uncertainty. It quantifies how many bits are needed, on average, to describe the next symbol.
  • English is ~75% redundant. Most of what we write is predictable from context, grammar, and world knowledge.
  • Humans are excellent language models. Shannon used human prediction to estimate entropy more accurately than any mathematical model of his era.
  • Context dramatically reduces entropy. The more you know about preceding text, the easier it is to predict what comes next.
  • Redundancy enables compression. Without predictable structure, compression would be impossible. Shannon's entropy sets the theoretical compression limit.
  • This paper prefigured modern AI. Next-symbol prediction, the core of Shannon's experiment, is the exact training objective of today's large language models.
