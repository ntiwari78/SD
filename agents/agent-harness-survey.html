Harness or Model? Same Order of Magnitude

An Evidence Audit of Agent-Harness Research, with 63 Paired Comparisons in Which Harness Choice and Model Choice Move Performance by Comparable Amounts

Abstract

A language model is not an agent. It becomes one through a harness: the system prompt, tool and skill definitions, execution environment, control loop, context and state management, verification gates, and orchestration logic that surround it. Since early 2026 the field has held a confident belief about this layer, namely that for long-horizon tasks the harness governs more performance variance than the choice of model, and has redirected engineering effort accordingly. We audit the evidence for that belief across 191 sources published between 2022 and October 2026, and find that the comparison it rests on had never been computed. So we compute it.

We first grade every substantive claim in the corpus on an evidence scale from E1 (replicated across independent controlled studies) to E4 (vendor or practitioner report), and on a deployment-maturity scale from M0 (proposed) to M4 (production default). Applying the evidence scale strictly to the numerator leaves four controlled same-model harness interventions, each from a primary study, moving scores by 7.0, 7.3, 10.1, and 28.9 percentage points. Applying it to the denominator removes every figure the literature circulates, because all of them come from practitioner posts rather than controlled studies. That is where previous accounts, including an earlier draft of this one, stopped.

Both sides were available the whole time. One standardised evaluation effort in our own corpus [74] ran seven of its benchmarks under two scaffolds each and published per-model accuracy tables, which yields two controlled quantities from identical runs. Holding the model and benchmark fixed and varying only the scaffold gives 63 paired harness interventions with a median effect of 9.0 percentage points and a maximum of 48.0, of which 31 exceed their benchmark's sampling noise. Holding the scaffold fixed and varying the model, restricted to frontier families and using standard deviation rather than a sample-size-inflated range, gives fifteen cells with a median model-side dispersion of 6.9 points. These are the same order of magnitude: the field's organising belief is not confirmed, and neither is its inverse. What we do find is an interaction. How much model choice matters depends on which harness is held fixed, by a median factor of 1.8× across six benchmarks and by 4.0× on SWE-bench Verified Mini, where seven frontier families span 10.0 points under SWE-Agent — one standard error, indistinguishable from no effect — and 46.0 points under a generic scaffold. We report four limits plainly: paired deltas and dispersions are different kinds of quantity, every cell is a single run, scaffold–model pairings are self-selected so harness architecture is confounded with tuning effort, and cost explains part of the effect on one benchmark though not across the 63 pairs, where the correlation is 0.03.

A second result emerges from grading the two scales against each other. Several mechanisms that are M4, meaning production defaults in widely deployed harnesses, rest on E3 or E4 evidence: context compaction policy, cross-session artifacts, initializer agents, and the shape of context degradation. We call this a maturity–evidence inversion and argue it identifies the highest-value controlled experiments available, because the interventions whose effects are already assumed are the ones nobody has isolated.

We claim neither the binding-constraint thesis nor the instruments for testing it. Zhang et al. [183] named the thesis and proposed both a disclosure standard and a variance-decomposition protocol; Section 1.1 states exactly what we take from them. Our contribution is the audit, the strict regrade, the paired computation, and the interaction result. Readers who maintain agent leaderboards, who are deciding whether to invest in model upgrades or harness engineering, or who review agent papers will find the computation directly actionable; readers seeking a component-by- component survey of harness design will find that in Section 5.

Keywords: agent harness; harness engineering; LLM agents; evidence grading; agent evaluation; model–harness confounding; reproducibility; measurement validity; research synthesis

1Introduction

A raw language model cannot maintain durable state, execute code, reach real-time knowledge, or set up an environment, yet agents do all four. The difference is supplied by code that is not the model: the loop that decides what happens next, the tools that turn a decision into an effect, the policy that decides what the model sees at each step, the sandbox that bounds the damage, and the verifier that decides whether the work is done. We call that layer the harness and define it as the model-external runtime that determines, at every step, what the model observes, what it may do, what is retained, when it stops, and what evidence the run leaves behind. Practitioners converged on the name in early 2026 and on a slogan for its scope: Agent = Model + Harness [141, 33, 109].

One ambiguity must be resolved before that definition is usable, because arguments about harnesses founder on it, and we resolve it here rather than later. Böckeler [18] distinguishes three senses: the model-level harness, meaning everything except the model; the builder harness, meaning what ships inside a coding-agent product; and the outer harness, meaning what a user builds around that product for their own codebase. Statements true of one are routinely false of another. Throughout this paper, unqualified “harness” means the model-level sense, and the comparison we compute is a model-level comparison: one model wrapped in different complete runtimes, against one runtime wrapping different models. Where the builder or outer sense is meant we say so.

The field converged on a belief about this layer, and that belief is the subject of this paper. The belief is that for long-horizon tasks, among models of comparable frontier capability, harness configuration governs more performance variance than model choice, so evaluation practice that reports one number per {model, benchmark} pair systematically misattributes harness gains to model improvements [183, 75]. The belief is plausible, widely repeated, and consequential: it redirects engineering effort away from model upgrades and toward runtime design. We set out to survey the evidence for it.

The comparison had never been computed, and when computed it comes out even. Grading every quantitative comparison in a 191-source corpus on a four-level evidence scale leaves four controlled same-model harness interventions, each from a primary study, moving primary-benchmark scores by 7.0, 7.3, 10.1, and 28.9 percentage points [171, 83, 85, 92], and removes every circulating figure for the inverse comparison, because all of them trace to practitioner posts [64, 128]. But the inverse comparison was obtainable from a source already in the corpus and already cited nine times in this paper. A standardised evaluation effort [74] ran seven benchmarks under two scaffolds each and published per-model accuracy tables, which yields both sides of the comparison from identical runs. Varying only the scaffold gives 63 paired harness interventions with a median effect of 9.0 points and a maximum of 48.0; varying only the model, under a fixed scaffold and restricted to frontier families, gives fifteen cells with a median dispersion of 6.9 points (Section 6.4, Tables Table 3 and Table 4). The two sides are the same order of magnitude. Neither the field's belief nor its inverse survives; what does is an interaction, because how much model choice matters turns out to depend on which harness is held fixed.

We are deliberate about how strong that makes the conclusion. It does not settle the question, for four reasons we develop in Section 6.4: a paired treatment effect and a population dispersion are different kinds of quantity, so their comparison bounds an order of magnitude rather than estimating a ratio; every cell is a single run, by the measuring authors' own admission; the scaffold–model pairings are self-selected, so harness architecture is confounded with benchmark-specific tuning effort; and a scaffold can buy accuracy with tokens, which explains part of the effect on one benchmark though not across the 63 pairs. What it does do is remove the empirical basis for both strong claims, and it establishes that the comparison is cheap rather than impossible. The 40,000 factorial that Section 8 prices is the way to settle it properly; an afternoon with published tables is enough to show which way it currently leans.

1.1Relation to prior work

Three things a reader might expect us to claim are not ours, and we state the attribution before the contributions rather than after. Zhang et al. [183] named the binding constraint thesis, defended it with a control-theoretic formalisation, and proposed both a harness disclosure standard and a variance-decomposition protocol. We adopt their thesis as the object of audit rather than as a result, we adopt their disclosure and decomposition proposals as the instruments the field needs, and Section 8 presents our extensions to both as future work rather than as contributions. Readers tracing priority on the thesis, the disclosure card, or the factorial protocol should cite that paper, not this one.

We also owe a specific debt to the measurement effort whose tables supply our denominator [74]. It built those tables deliberately, observing that across its benchmark set “only 2 of these benchmarks were ever evaluated with the same agent scaffold for 4 or more models,” which is the gap it set out to close. Our contribution on that point is narrow and we state it narrowly: we noticed that the tables answer a question the harness literature was posing elsewhere, and we did the subtraction. We are not reporting a new experiment.

Finally, the decomposition we use to organise Section 5 is not offered as a new taxonomy. Three substantial harness taxonomies appeared independently in 2026 [63, 118, 50] and two further decompositions circulate informally [57, 186]. A field with five competing decompositions does not need a sixth. We use a plain five-stage-plus-three-plane decomposition as an organising device, supply a crosswalk so any reader can translate our section structure into whichever taxonomy they already use (Table Table 1), and make no claim that ours is correct.

This is not a protocol-driven systematic review. 2Scope, Corpus, and Method

2.1Corpus construction

The corpus comprises 191 retrieved documents, and because we did not run a database search we report the retrieval flow exactly rather than presenting it as a protocol. Candidate references were pooled from three independently produced literature maps of the agent-harness area, contributing 120, 87, and 13 links respectively, for 220 candidate link references. Of those, 206 resolved, every one with HTTP 200; 14 were excluded, every one with HTTP 403 from a bot or CDN wall. After deduplicating targets that several maps cited in common, 15 link rows collapsed, leaving 191 unique documents, comprising 143 PDFs and 48 web documents. Three were unusable, leaving 188 coded sources, all of which are cited. Coverage runs from the publication of ReAct in 2022 [174] to October 2026, and 140 of the retrieved documents carry arXiv identifiers.

Two properties of this flow are worth stating because they bound what follows. It is a convenience sample: pooling three literature maps gives no recall guarantee, and a source absent from all three is absent from this paper. But it is an auditable convenience sample, since every candidate link, its HTTP status, its failure reason where applicable, and its resulting local filename are recorded in a machine-readable manifest that reconciles exactly to the retrieved file set.

2.2Inclusion and exclusion

We included a source if it addressed at least one Facet as a first-class concern, or supplied evidence about how such a facet affects agent behaviour. This admits four classes that we keep distinct throughout: peer-reviewed and archival papers; 2026 preprints, which form the majority of the harness-specific literature and are largely unreviewed; vendor and practitioner documents; and community reference material. We excluded three retrieved files as unusable: one mathematics paper on surface-knot modules, retrieved because a cited arXiv identifier resolved to a real but unrelated work, and two bot-challenge pages returned in place of documents.

2.3Known gaps

Three gaps bear on specific claims and we flag them where they arise. First, the primary PDF of one of the three principal harness surveys could not be retrieved, because the hosting preprint server blocked automated access; our account of its etcslv framework therefore rests on its official repository and on a secondary synthesis [118, 6]. Second, ten further candidate sources returned HTTP 403 from bot or CDN walls, including four from one preprint server. Four blocked ResearchGate links are fully covered by their arXiv originals, which are in the corpus. Third, several widely deployed systems have documentation rather than papers, including LangGraph, CrewAI, the OpenAI Agents SDK, the Claude Agent SDK, and the agent-to-agent protocol; we cite their documentation and label it as practitioner material [82, 129].

2.4Evidence and maturity grading

Agent-harness claims arrive with wildly uneven support, and no existing survey separates a replicated controlled finding from a figure quoted in a product launch. We therefore grade on two independent axes. The evidence axis measures how well a claim is established:

  • E1 Replicated. Supported by two or more independent controlled studies.
  • E2 Single controlled study. One study that holds confounders fixed, typically by fixing the model and varying the harness, or by ablation.
  • E3 Observational. Leaderboard comparisons, comparisons across systems without matched conditions, or correlational evidence.
  • E4 Reported only. Vendor or practitioner report, or a figure circulating second-hand without a primary source.

The maturity axis measures how far a mechanism has travelled into practice:

  • M0 Proposed in writing, not implemented.
  • M1 Research prototype, single-paper implementation.
  • M2 Open-source implementation used beyond its authors.
  • M3 Documented production use.
  • M4 Production default in a widely deployed harness.

The two axes are not correlated, and their divergence is informative. A mechanism graded M4/E4 is running in front of many users on the strength of no controlled study, which is a research opportunity rather than a criticism of the engineers who shipped it.

2.5Use of AI assistance

Large language models were used in producing this paper, and because that bears directly on the bibliographic integrity a reader should demand, we state where and what was checked.

Three stages used model assistance. Candidate literature identification was performed by language models, which produced the three literature maps that seeded the corpus; no database search was run. Retrieval, text extraction, per-source summarisation, and the mechanical parts of grading were scripted, with the model-assisted components reviewed by the author. Drafting was model-assisted throughout, with all claims checked against the retrieved sources by the author.

The risk this creates is specific: a model-generated reference list can contain identifiers that are fabricated, mis-transcribed, or attributed to the wrong work. Two mitigations were applied. First, every cited source was retrieved and is held locally, so no citation in this paper rests on a reference the author has not obtained. Second, a stratified random sample of 41 of the 140 arXiv identifiers (29%), drawn across all year prefixes in the corpus, was resolved live against arxiv.org and the returned titles compared with the bibliography. All 41 matched exactly; there were no mismatches and no retrieval failures. One further detection is worth recording: a candidate identifier in one literature map resolved to a real but entirely unrelated mathematics paper, which is precisely the failure mode this check is designed to catch, and it was excluded as one of the three unusable documents.

Two residual risks survive and we do not claim otherwise. Title matching establishes that a cited paper exists and is the paper named; it does not establish that the paper supports the claim made of it, and no sampling procedure can verify that at scale. And a 29% sample bounds the rate of identifier error without eliminating the possibility of an error outside the sample.

2.6Threats to the validity of this survey

Three threats deserve statement up front. First, 2026 preprint density: the harness-specific literature is recent, fast-moving, and sometimes revised under new titles, so individual figures may not survive peer review, and we mark version-dependent numbers where we are aware of them. Second, our own grading is a judgement, applied by one reading rather than by multiple independent annotators, and so inherits the single-reviewer weakness that better-designed reviews avoid; the most methodologically rigorous source in the corpus, a PRISMA systematic review, validated its screening at κ = 0.81 and still graded seven of its nine principal claims at the lowest certainty level [31]. Third, publication and promotion bias runs strongly toward positive harness results, since a harness modification that changed nothing is rarely written up; the one study designed to detect exactly that outcome found it [21].

3From Prompt Engineering to Harness Engineering

3.1Four paradigms

Agent engineering has passed through four overlapping paradigms, and naming them explains why the harness became visible only recently. The first was prompt engineering, where the unit of design was a string. The second was workflow and context engineering, where it became a sequence of model calls with managed inputs. The third, current paradigm is harness engineering, where the unit of design is the runtime that situates a model in a closed loop. The fourth, emerging, is agent-native training with model–harness co-evolution, in which weights and runtime are optimised jointly [50, 91]. Each paradigm widened the scope of what counts as designable, and the harness became a research object at the point where its design decisions began to outweigh prompt-level ones.

3.2Terminology and its history

The same layer has been named four times. METR called it scaffolding in 2023, in the formula scaffolding plus model equals agent. The SWE-agent authors named a specific part of it the agent–computer interface in 2024 and demonstrated that redesigning it moved benchmark performance with no change to weights [171]. Practitioners and vendors settled on harness in early 2026 [141, 18, 1]. Academic surveys followed within months [63, 50]. The multiplicity matters because it fragments the literature: work on context compaction, tool schema design, sandboxing, and trajectory logging addresses one system but cites four disjoint bibliographies.

3.3Bounded contexts of the word

Arguments about harnesses frequently founder because the word means three different things. The distinction is due to Böckeler [18] and is worth preserving. The model-level harness is everything except the model, which is the broad vendor definition. The builder harness is what ships inside a coding-agent product, such as its system prompt, retrieval mechanism, and orchestration. The outer harness is what a user builds around that product for their own codebase, such as custom linters, hooks, and repository instructions. Statements true of one are often false of another: the claim that harness design is a user-facing discipline is true of the outer harness and false of the model-level harness. We state which context is meant wherever confusion is possible.

3.4Why three taxonomies coexist

No definition of the harness has won, and the three surveys published in 2026 decompose it differently because they were written for different purposes. One organises by engineering layer and maps open-source projects onto it [63]. One formalises the harness as a tuple with labelled-transition-system semantics, separating safety from liveness properties [118]. One organises by runtime responsibility within a model–harness lens [50]. Two further decompositions circulate: an eleven-responsibility account with an H0–H3 maturity ladder [186], and a deliberately loose working usage in an empirical study of 70 projects [57]. A survey that merely listed all five would leave the fragmentation in place. Section Section 4 therefore proposes a single spine and accepts the obligation to map the others onto it.

4An Organising Decomposition

4.1What the decomposition is for

We defined the harness in Section 1 as the model-external runtime that determines, at every step, what the model observes, what it may do, what is retained, when it stops, and what evidence the run leaves behind. That definition is functional rather than architectural, so a harness implemented as a command-line loop, a graph runtime, a gateway control plane, or an editable natural-language document [107] all fall inside it.

One ambiguity has to be resolved before the definition is usable, because arguments about harnesses frequently founder on it. Böckeler [18] distinguishes three senses: the model-level harness, meaning everything except the model; the builder harness, meaning what ships inside a coding-agent product; and the outer harness, meaning what a user builds around that product for their own codebase. Statements true of one are often false of another. Throughout this paper, unqualified “harness” means the model-level sense, and the comparison audited in Section 6 is a model-level comparison: the same model wrapped in different complete runtimes. Where the builder or outer sense is meant we say so.

The decomposition below separates that runtime into a pipeline of five stages traversed once per step and three planes that apply at every stage. We use it to order Section 5 and for nothing else. The one substantive point it encodes is the pipeline/plane distinction: a permission check is not a phase of the loop but a constraint on every phase, and a trace is not a component but a projection of all of them, which the existing taxonomies obscure by listing verification, governance, and observability alongside context and tools as peer layers. We deliberately give the stages and planes plain names and no acronym, because the field already has five acronyms for this layer and a sixth would be a cost rather than a contribution.

4.2The five pipeline stages

The five pipeline stages follow the causal order of a single agent step.

Observation.
How environment output becomes model input. Covers rendering, truncation, filtering, and projection of tool results, screenshots, and file contents. Distinct from context because it concerns the transformation of a single observation rather than the composition of the window.
Context.
What occupies the window at each step. Covers budgeting, compaction, summarisation, retrieval, and re-injection policy.
Control.
How the next action is chosen and when the run ends. Covers loop structure, planning, search, delegation and sub-agent spawning, retries, and stopping rules.
Action.
How a decision becomes an effect. Covers the tool interface and its schemas, code execution, protocol layers, and the execution environment as the medium of effect.
State.
What persists outside the window. Covers session state, task state, cross-session artifacts, and externalised working memory.

4.3The three planes

Verification.
Establishing that a step or a task is actually complete. Covers tests, executable checks, auditors, and the separation of doing from certifying.
Governance.
Permissions, approval gates, sandbox boundaries, and policy enforcement, including which instructions are allowed to direct behaviour.
Observability.
Traces, provenance, cost accounting, and the evidence a run leaves for debugging, evaluation, optimisation, and audit.

4.4Where the execution environment sits

Two of the three existing taxonomies give the execution environment its own top-level layer, and we deliberately do not. In this decomposition the sandbox appears twice, as the medium of Action, which determines what effects are possible, and as an instrument of Governance, which determines which are permitted. Splitting it this way is a substantive choice, and it is justified by the security literature: the empirically important failures are not sandbox escapes but mismatches between what the environment permits and what the policy intends [54, 73]. A model that keeps capability and permission in one box cannot express that mismatch. The cost of the choice is that readers comparing this decomposition with etclovg must look in two places for E, which Table Table 1 makes explicit.

4.5Crosswalk

Three asymmetries in Table Table 1 are worth naming because each marks a real disagreement rather than a notational difference. The first is whether state is separable from context: the seven-layer taxonomy folds them together, while the tuple formalisation separates them, and the strongest long-horizon result in the corpus depends on separating them, since it works by moving task state out of the growing context entirely [92]. The second is whether governance is distinct from verification: merging them, as one taxonomy does, makes it hard to state the finding that a permission reviewer can correctly identify a dangerous action and approve it anyway [54]. The third is observability, which one taxonomy omits and which no examined system implements in externally verifiable form.

5Facets of the Harness

This section surveys each Facet, states the mechanisms the literature offers, and grades the central claim of each subsection.

5.1Observation

Observation is the least theorised facet and the one with the clearest recent mechanism. The problem is that raw environment output is frequently unusable: real-world HTML is too large to feed a model, and filtering it first with a small language model improves both effectiveness and efficiency [37]. The same logic recurs wherever the environment is verbose. Full-file dumps destroy context, so a bounded file viewer is substituted; a search that returns whole matches degrades downstream tool selection, so it returns filenames only [8].

The 2026 formulation makes observation a learned function rather than a heuristic. HarnessBridge parameterises the agent–environment interface as a bidirectional projection whose observation half classifies every piece of incoming state as pass, compress, or drop, maintaining an active-state index of unresolved errors, open constraints, established facts, and pending goals [148]. Its action half is the mirror image, converting proposed actions into executable transitions or trajectory-grounded rejections. Reported outcomes match or exceed specialised harnesses on two benchmarks with substantially lower token usage and shorter trajectories. E2 M1

5.2Context

The context window is the only space in which reasoning occurs, and three independent costs make naive accumulation untenable. Cost grows quadratically in conversation length because each step reprocesses the whole history; latency grows linearly in context length; and accuracy degrades continuously rather than failing at the limit, with reported losses above 30 percentage points for content in the middle of long contexts [41, 48]. Bigger windows do not dissolve the problem, since long-context models underuse the context they are given [106].

Five mechanism families address this, and a survey of agent memory organises them as a write–manage–read loop over three dimensions of temporal scope, representational substrate, and control policy [114]. The families are context-resident compression; retrieval-augmented external stores; reflective self-improvement [136]; hierarchical virtual context, whose canonical instance pages between a main context and external storage in deliberate imitation of operating-system virtual memory [106]; and policy-learned management. The best-supported recent entry treats context as an evolving playbook refined by generation, reflection, and curation, specifically to defeat two failure modes it names: brevity bias, which discards domain insight for concise summaries, and context collapse, where iterative rewriting erodes detail [181]. E2 M2

Compaction is lossy, and the practical consensus is that durable state belongs outside the transcript [13, 64]. One production harness re-injects project memory files after compaction; a graph runtime separates short-term checkpointers from long-term stores [82]. An agent-controlled variant lets the model decide when to consolidate learnings and prune history, reporting a 22.7% token reduction at identical accuracy, though on a five-instance sample that cannot support more than a directional claim [103]. A lifecycle framing decomposes the discipline into architecting, ingesting, scoping, anticipating, and compacting, and supplies the economic argument that matters: naive accumulation is quadratic, crude summarisation buys linear cost at the price of an accuracy cliff, and only validated compaction achieves linear cost with preserved fidelity [48]. E3 M4

The methodological caution on this facet is strong enough to qualify every claim above. Agent working memory is semantically heterogeneous: instructions, source artifacts, tool outputs, and agent-generated state have different size, retention, and compression profiles, yet most policies treat them uniformly [27]. Across 55 archived coding-agent trajectories, that study reports two results that invalidate common comparisons: calibration gains may not transfer to held-out tasks, and equal token budgets do not imply equal delivered context or equal management cost. It proposes evaluating at four levels, namely stored state, delivered context, management work, and task outcome. E2 M1

5.3Control

Every production agent in 2026 runs a variant of the loop published in 2022, in which reasoning tokens interleave with tool calls and observations until a stopping condition fires [174, 11]. Four families extend it, and each is a harness decision rather than a model change: verbal self-correction, which converts environment feedback into text that acts as a semantic gradient [136, 94]; decoupled planning, which separates plan from execution; deliberate search, which replaces the linear trajectory with a tree under Monte Carlo tree search and learned value functions [188, 14]; and resource-managed execution, which isolates scheduling, memory, and access control into a kernel beneath agent applications [96].

The structural choice within control is whether to express the loop as free-form code or as an explicit state machine. A hand-written while loop has no hooks, so it cannot be paused, rewound, or branched, and past a demonstration the agent becomes a black box that either worked or did not [10]. The alternative observes that the agent already is a state machine, comprising system prompt, message history, pending tool calls, and next action, and makes that explicit as a graph. Doing so yields four capabilities for free: checkpointing at every node transition, interrupts for human approval, streaming of intermediate events, and time-travel to retry a different branch [10, 82]. The agent is unchanged; the harness is not. E3 M4

The most interesting control finding is architectural convergence under opposing design philosophies. A source-level study read three open coding-agent harnesses at pinned commits and through their commit histories, and found the two mature ones had travelled in opposite directions, one subtracting authored scaffolding and the other accreting durable infrastructure, yet both converged on one middle form of five elements: a commoditised loop, an append-only replayable session record, model quirks kept as data rather than code, progressive disclosure of context, and explicit extension seams [66]. A third harness read as a held-out check exhibited all five, and in one seam reused another's implementation outright, so the study decomposes the convergence honestly into parallel discovery, diffusion, and literal reuse. E2 M3

Delegation belongs to control, and the 2026 literature is markedly more sceptical about it than the 2023 wave. Role specialisation with standard operating procedures was introduced to defeat cascading hallucinations, in which an early wrong assumption propagates through every later stage of a naive agent chain [55], and the orchestrator-plus-specialists pattern that most production harnesses now use was established shortly after [44]. Three subsequent results complicate the picture. A failure taxonomy built from 150 expert-annotated traces at κ = 0.88, with a dataset of over 1,600 traces across seven frameworks, identifies 14 failure modes in three categories, namely system design, inter-agent misalignment, and task verification, and opens by noting that multi-agent gains on popular benchmarks are often minimal [25]. A controlled comparison finds that a single agent in multi-turn conversation matches homogeneous multi-agent workflows with an efficiency advantage from key-value cache reuse, and even matches an automatically optimised heterogeneous workflow [167]. A controlled benchmark characterising tasks along depth, horizon, breadth, parallelism, and robustness finds gains depend on task structure, verification protocol, and the capability of both orchestrator and sub-agents [76]. E1 M3

A decision-theoretic result bounds the whole area. Modelling multi-agent planning as a finite acyclic decision network whose stages share context and communicate through limited-capacity language interfaces, any delegated network is dominated by a centralised Bayes decision maker with the same information, absent new exogenous signals [15]. Delegation cannot add information; it can only lose it through communication and compression, and the loss admits an expected posterior divergence representation. Multi-agent systems therefore earn their keep through parallelism, specialisation of deployment, or access to signals a single agent lacks, not through decomposition as such.

5.4Action

The action facet produced the field's most quoted result and its clearest statement of leverage. Treating language-model agents as a new category of end user with their own perceptual limits, the agent–computer interface thesis holds that an agent cannot scan a screen, has no visual working memory, reads tokens sequentially, and loses attention as context grows, so the tools it uses should be designed for that reader [171, 20]. A custom interface supporting repository navigation, bounded file viewing, line editing, search, and structured environment feedback reached state of the art on two benchmarks with no change to model weights. The leverage argument follows directly: the model is approximately fixed, while every tool the agent sees can be redesigned this afternoon. E1 M4

Mistake-proofing is reported as the highest-yield technique within this facet, because a single constraint change can eliminate an entire failure class. Documented instances include a bounded file viewer that stopped context loss from full dumps, a search returning filenames only that improved downstream tool selection, a syntax-validating linter that blocked cascading failures, and explicit empty-state messages replacing silent returns [8]. The general mapping from interface design to agent design is systematic rather than metaphorical: affordances become tool descriptions, constraints become typed and validated parameters, feedback becomes semantic output with explicit empty states, and error prevention becomes validation before effect.

The necessary counterweight is that interface quality and control rigidity trade off. An approach that discards the agent loop entirely for a fixed three-phase pipeline of localisation, repair, and patch validation, with no model-decided control flow and no complex tools, achieved both the best performance and the lowest cost on a widely used benchmark subset at the time of publication [161]. Sometimes the best harness is a rigid pipeline rather than a richer tool surface. That same work also found benchmark instances with ground-truth patches leaked into the issue description, and released a filtered variant, which anticipates the construct-validity concerns of Section 7.

A distinct 2026 position holds that the substrate of action should be code rather than a tool schema. Agents emitting JSON or templated text face a constrained action space bounded by predefined tools and cannot compose multiple tools in one step; consolidating actions into executable code gives a unified action space with composition, branching, and revision, and reportedly improves success by up to 20% across 17 models [143]. A 102-page survey organises the position into three layers of harness interface, harness mechanisms, and harness scaling, and frames the key property as failures becoming machine-readable feedback for repair rather than dead ends [104, 158]. The complementary efficiency argument is concrete: as agents connect to thousands of tools, loading all definitions upfront can consume hundreds of thousands of tokens before the request is read, and every intermediate result passes through context on each hop, both of which code execution avoids [12]. E2 M3

Two sources push in the opposite direction, and the disagreement is genuine rather than apparent. Natural-language agent harnesses externalise run-level harness policy as editable documents interpreted by a shared runtime, on the grounds that a code harness bundles prompts, tool adapters, parser rules, validation scripts, retry logic, and benchmark assumptions together, so a small change silently alters call boundaries and stopping semantics [107]. Harness distillation reports that an agent-as-harness arrangement outperforms code-as-harness for frontier models on the same evolved harness [175]. Both camps claim better inspectability; they disagree about which substrate delivers it.

The protocol layer beneath action is dominated by one standard and is the least secure part of the modern harness. The Model Context Protocol uses a host–client–server architecture over JSON-RPC and exposes three primitives: server-provided prompt templates, read-only resources, and executable tools whose own specification warns that they represent arbitrary code execution [29, 56]. Its defining property, and the root of its security problems, is that it exposes rich pre-execution artifacts such as tool names, natural-language descriptions, argument schemas, and server-provided prompts, which enter the model's context and directly influence tool selection [125]. The description is itself an input to the decision, which is what makes tool poisoning possible (Section 9). A survey of interoperability protocols positions it alongside three alternatives and proposes a phased adoption path from tool access, through structured multimodal messaging, to peer-to-peer delegation and open-network discovery [42].

5.5State

State is the facet where the strongest long-horizon result in the corpus lives, and the mechanism is simple enough to state in one sentence: take task state out of the growing context and update it only from independently verified environment facts. The diagnosis is that existing harnesses keep execution, task state, and completion assessment inside one expanding context, which makes state hard to track and allows incorrect self-assessments to propagate into later decisions [92]. The remedy is a manage–execute–audit loop in which a manager holds state and chooses the next subtask, a fresh-context executor performs it, and a read-only auditor certifies what actually changed, with audit reports as the only cross-round memory. Reported gains are large and transfer across models and harnesses, including 51.8% to 80.7% on one benchmark, 69.7% to 77.2% on a second, and a threefold improvement on a third. E2 M1

Externalising state also simplifies learning. A search agent trained with reinforcement learning inside a stateful harness argues that training a policy over growing transcripts forces the optimiser to handle both semantic search decisions and recoverable bookkeeping that the environment could maintain more reliably, so the harness keeps a candidate pool, an importance-tagged curated set, evidence links, verification records, deduplicated observations, and budget-aware rendering, while the policy keeps only semantic decisions [68]. The reported improvement is largest on held-out transfer benchmarks, which is the pattern one expects if the harness is absorbing task-general machinery rather than task-specific tricks.

Cross-session state is where maturity and evidence diverge most sharply. The problem statement is vivid and widely accepted: agents work in discrete sessions, each beginning with no memory of the last, like a project staffed by engineers working shifts with no handover [13]. The documented remedies, namely an initializer agent that prepares the environment once and a working agent that makes incremental progress while leaving artifacts for its successor, are deployed at scale but rest on vendor engineering reports rather than controlled studies. E4 M4

5.6Verification

Verification is the plane that separates doing from certifying, and the literature's strongest claim is that execution feedback is a better supervision signal than model likelihood. One multi-agent framework makes execution-grounded verification a first-class principle, requiring every code change to survive sandboxed execution before it propagates, in contrast to systems that infer execution outcomes or treat verification as optional, and its ablations show execution feedback and role decomposition contributing independently [81]. The same principle appears in the code-as-harness framing, where tests, traces, and static analysis are the mechanism by which failures become repairable rather than terminal [104].

Formal verification of agent plans, by contrast, is further from practice than its literature suggests. A PRISMA systematic review of 38 studies, with per-study risk-of-bias assessment and GRADE certainty, reports four findings that bound the area [31]. Specification is the bottleneck, with only 24.8% to 34.2% of model translations from natural language into a planning domain definition language being semantically correct. Runtime monitoring is the most mature enforcement strategy, reducing unsafe actions by 65.4% in one study, but its guarantees cover only single steps or short horizons. There is a verifier tax: blocking 94% of unsafe actions still left fewer than 5% of tasks completed both safely and correctly in most settings, because agents found other unsafe routes. And no approach simultaneously achieves soundness, completeness, scalability, semantic grounding, and utility preservation. Seven of its nine principal claims are graded at the lowest certainty level. E1 M1

5.7Governance

Governance is structurally different from conventional application security because an agent generates and executes novel code at run time from inputs that may be attacker-controlled, makes resource and API decisions that static policy cannot anticipate, and maintains persistent memory vulnerable to manipulation across sessions [17]. The empirical hook is that safety mechanisms built for conversational use do not transfer: a model deployed as an autonomous agent reportedly executed injection, server-side request forgery, and unauthorised exfiltration that its chat-only counterpart refused. An empirical study of 70 agent projects found that intermediate isolation is common while high-assurance audit is rare [57]. E3 M3

Runtime guardrails have moved from reactive to predictive, and the critique motivating the shift is precise. Most guardrails assess only the apparent safety of the current proposed action, with no model of how risk evolves along the trajectory, which leaves a blind spot for long-horizon risk where individually benign actions drift the agent toward a hazardous state [86]. The proposed remedy maintains a recurrent latent state over the trajectory, predicts future states, and fuses immediate-hazard with prefix-risk evidence before execution at roughly 25 milliseconds per call. A complementary line optimises the guardrail harness itself around trigger, monitor, and feedback modules, and concludes that effective runtime defence requires complementary mechanisms, attack-aware optimisation, and harness designs matched to monitor capability [87]. E2 M1

The most useful governance result for practitioners is that enforcement must live in code rather than in prompts, and it is supported by a clean ablation. Holding the model fixed and varying only the enforcement layer, prompt instructions alone allowed recommendation-language and internal-trace leakage violations to reach the reader, which a code-owned harness blocked entirely; a bolt-on external guardrail also blocked them but over-refused, dropping utility to 88 of 120 where the harness preserved 120 of 120 [9]. The contracts also survived model substitution, passing all 270 composition-boundary runs across three hosted models. Only code-owned enforcement preserved both safety and utility. E2 M2

Formal and evolving policy is the 2026 research direction, and it resolves a real dilemma. Adaptive guardrails learn from deployment but apply policy through model inference, giving inconsistent decisions and ambiguous interpretation, whereas formal guardrails enforce deterministically but rely on pre-deployment policy with no way to adapt [101]. The proposed synthesis represents the guardrail as executable symbolic rules over typed agent events, has a model propose policy patches from deployment feedback, admits only structurally valid and conflict-free policies through a deterministic verifier, and activates a verified rule only once it has sufficient supporting evidence. The same logic applied to regulation treats compliance as runtime verification over the event trace, on the sharp observation that because tool selection, argument generation, and disclosure are all model-controlled at run time, the same deployment can be compliant in one dialogue and not in the next with no change to code, prompt, or weights [72]. E2 M1

5.8Observability

Agents fail in a way conventional observability cannot detect, and the reason is worth stating precisely: a hallucination returns HTTP 200, and a run that took fourteen needless tool calls looks identical to a clean one [126]. Logs stay clean, latency looks healthy, error rates sit at zero, and a user eventually reports the problem. Two decades of monitoring practice assume misbehaving software leaves evidence in the form of an exception, a timeout, or a bad status code, and agents break that assumption.

The structural insight that makes instrumentation tractable is that an agent run is already shaped like a trace. A run contains steps, each step contains a model call and some tool calls, and tool calls sometimes call further services, which is a tree of nested timed operations and therefore exactly what distributed tracing represents [117]. The corollary is that the problem is correlation rather than log volume: adding log lines to a system with no correlation produces more disconnected lines to stitch. A mature span contract carries session and parent session, provenance attributes identifying which agent acted and which model ran, what was consumed and generated, and the cost, which makes the decision chain the first-class artifact rather than the final output [2]. E3 M3

Observing is not sufficient, because existing tooling shows what the agent did rather than whether it was any good, and cannot intervene while a run is going wrong [126]. The response is a single pipeline that collects, filters, evaluates, and acts, with the loop-closing detail that production failures export directly into evaluation cases so real failures become regression tests. Observability is also load-bearing for two other facets: trace-driven harness evolution is impossible without it (Section 10), and log analysis is a precondition for credible evaluation rather than an operational convenience (Section 7).

The gap that no examined system fills is external verifiability. Mature harnesses converge on an append-only replayable session record, but a tamper-evident record that an outside party can check without trusting the runtime is absent from all three harnesses examined at source level, and the study that found this reads the absence as a predictive gap rather than an oversight: it is the next axis on which harnesses for provenance-sensitive domains will differ [66]. Internal tracing serves debugging and optimisation. It does not serve audit. E2 M0

6The Audit and the Strict Regrade

6.1The belief under audit

The claim we audit was named and defended by Zhang et al. [183] as the binding constraint thesis: for long-horizon tasks evaluated across models of comparable frontier capability, the execution harness is often a stronger determinant of performance than the model it wraps, so current protocols systematically misattribute harness-level gains to model improvements. Their defence has three parts: a control-theoretic formalisation treating the harness as the controller of a closed-loop system and the model as the stochastic policy it governs; evidence that harness-induced variance exceeds model-induced variance, including cases of model ranking reversal; and a proposed disclosure standard with a variance-decomposition protocol. It is a position paper, and it states that its own controlled factorial is small.

Our question is narrower than theirs and purely evidential. Taking the corpus as it stands, what grade does each quantitative comparison actually earn under a scale applied consistently, and what survives? The answer turns out to depend almost entirely on one methodological commitment that the literature states but does not enforce.

6.2What the comparisons look like before regrading

Table Table 2 is the whole evidential base for the comparison, and read as originally graded it looks supportive. Seven rows report harness-varied spreads from 7.0 to 18.4 points, two rows report model-varied spreads of 10.2 and 1.3 points, and the natural reading is that the harness column dominates the model column. That reading is how the belief propagated.

6.3The strict regrade

Our method section commits to a rule we now apply to ourselves: E4 denotes a vendor or practitioner report, and we do not treat vendor engineering posts as evidence (Section 2.4, Section 2). Three rows in Table Table 2 have no primary source. One practitioner guide [64] is the sole source for the 18.4-point harness spread and for the 10.2-point model spread, and a vendor blog [128] is the sole source for the 1.3-point model spread. Demoting those rows to E4 has an asymmetric effect, and the asymmetry is the finding.

  • The numerator survives, reduced. Four controlled same-model interventions remain at E2, each from a primary study: 7.0 [171], 7.3 [85], 10.1 [83], and 28.9 points [92]. The controlled range is 7.0 to 28.9 percentage points.
  • Every circulating denominator fails. Both inverse comparisons that the harness literature quotes are vendor-only, so after regrading none of the figures the field uses as a comparator survives. This is where earlier accounts, including an earlier version of this paper, concluded that the comparison could not be made. That conclusion was wrong, and Section 6.4 shows why: the quantity was obtainable from a source already in this corpus.

Three consequences follow, and we state them in descending order of confidence. First, the comparison as the literature states it rests on no controlled evidence on either side, which is a weaker and different claim from the comparison being impossible. Second, the single largest unsupported figure is the one that does not survive: the 18.4-point harness spread has no source but one practitioner guide, which is cited ten times in this paper and is among its most-cited sources. The 16.7-point figure is co-sourced to a primary benchmark paper [97] and stays at E3, which is why Table Table 2 leaves it undemoted. Third, and least comfortably for anyone wanting a clean story, that same practitioner guide supplies the strongest counter-evidence too, namely that matched reasoning effort collapses harness spreads to between 0.2 and 8.1 points. Demoting the source removes the best argument for the belief and the best argument against it in one stroke. Both directions are currently unsupported.

A fair objection is that three of the four surviving E2 values, 7.0, 7.3, and 10.1, sit at or below 10.2, so a reader might conclude the harness effect is simply modest. We do not draw that conclusion, because 10.2 is itself the demoted figure and cannot serve as a threshold; and because the four surviving values come from only three distinct benchmarks, two of them sharing one, so their range reflects intervention and task heterogeneity as much as harness leverage. The honest statement is that controlled harness interventions reliably move long-horizon benchmark scores by several points and sometimes by a great deal more, and that nobody has yet measured the comparable quantity for models under a fixed harness.

6.4Computing the comparison

Both sides of the comparison were available the whole time, in a source this paper already cites nine times. HAL [74] ran 21,730 agent rollouts across nine models and nine benchmarks, released all 2.5 billion tokens of logs, and published per-model accuracy tables organised by scaffold. Seven of its benchmarks were run under two scaffolds each. That means the same model can be read off the same benchmark under two different harnesses, which is a controlled harness intervention; and models can be read off one fixed scaffold, which is a controlled estimate of model spread. An earlier draft of this paper read only the second of those and used the wrong statistic for it. This section reports both, and the two together give a different answer from either alone.

The harness side, paired

A paired comparison holds the model and the benchmark fixed and varies only the scaffold. Each pair is therefore a treatment effect in the same sense as the interventions in Table Table 2, measured on identical tasks in the same evaluation campaign. Sixty-three such pairs exist across seven benchmarks.

Paired harness effects have a median of 9.0 percentage points and a maximum of 48.0, and just under half of them (31 of 63) are larger than the sampling noise of their benchmark. This both corrects and extends Table Table 2: the four interventions that survived our regrade there (7.0, 7.3, 10.1, 28.9) are consistent with this distribution, but they understate its upper tail, and they come from four separate papers whereas these 63 come from one campaign with a common protocol.

The model side, under a fixed harness

The complementary quantity holds the scaffold fixed and varies the model. Two instrument choices matter here, and an earlier draft of this paper got both wrong.

First, the statistic. A best-minus-worst range grows with the number of contestants: across these cells the correlation between range and model count is 0.62, so a range is partly an estimator of sampling intensity rather than of model sensitivity. We report standard deviation instead, which does not inflate with n.

Second, the population. The belief under audit is explicitly about “models of comparable frontier capability” (Section 6.1). A range computed over a pool that includes small, open-weight, and year-old models is not a test of it; and in such pools the worst cell is routinely a model that could not operate the scaffold at all, which is a harness failure booked as model spread. On SWE-bench Verified Mini, o3 Medium scores 0.0 under HAL Generalist and 46.0 under SWE-Agent — a 46-point swing for the same model, which an unrestricted range would record as evidence that models differ. We therefore restrict to frontier families and collapse reasoning-effort variants to the best score per family. Fifteen benchmark–scaffold cells then retain at least four frontier families. Across those fifteen, model-side standard deviation has a median of 6.9 points and a range of 2.3 to 16.3.

The two sides, and the interaction between them

Set the two against each other in the same units, from the same runs: harness-side paired effects have a median of 9.0 points; model-side dispersion under a fixed harness has a median standard deviation of 6.9 points. They are the same order of magnitude, and neither dominates. The field's organising belief is not confirmed, and the inversion an earlier draft of this paper claimed is not supported either.

What does hold, and is the more interesting result, is that the two interact: how much model choice matters depends on which harness you hold fixed.

The clearest case is SWE-bench Verified Mini. The same seven frontier families span 10.0 points under SWE-Agent and 46.0 points under HAL Generalist, a four-fold difference in dispersion. Under a scaffold built for the benchmark, frontier model choice is statistically indistinguishable from no effect at all — 10.0 points is 1.0 standard error on fifty tasks — while under a generic scaffold the same models spread across nearly the whole range. On that benchmark the harness does not merely matter more than the model; it determines whether the model matters.

We are deliberate about how far that generalises, because it does not generalise far. The median interaction ratio across the six benchmarks is 1.8× and two benchmarks show essentially none (1.1×). SWE-bench Verified Mini is the extreme case, not the typical one. The defensible claim is that harness and model effects are comparable in magnitude, that they interact, and that the strength of the interaction is benchmark-dependent and in one well-studied case very large.

Four limits on how far this goes

Treatment effects and dispersions are different quantities. The two columns we set against each other are not the same kind of number. A paired delta is a counterfactual on one system: this model, these tasks, that scaffold instead of this one. A standard deviation is a property of a sample of models somebody else assembled. Calling one “larger” than the other is a statement about orders of magnitude, not an estimate of a ratio, and no arithmetic relation between them licenses a stronger reading. We record that Table Table 2, in its published form, placed both kinds of number in a single column headed “Spread” — our own reproduction of an error that pervades this literature.

No intervals. Every cell is a single run, as the measuring authors state: “we were forced to rely on single runs without statistical validation for most evaluations.” We have bounded this where it bites hardest by computing binomial sampling error from each benchmark's task count — that is the “Sig.” column of Table Table 3, and it is why we report that just under half the paired deltas, not all of them, exceed noise. It is a bound, not a substitute for repeats. The complementary study [120] runs 15 models through one scaffold per benchmark at five seeds and temperature zero, which meets the repeat bar Section 8 recommends, but reports accuracy only graphically, so intervals would require its released per-seed data rather than its paper.

Scaffold–model pairings are self-selected. SWE-Agent was built and tuned for SWE-bench; the HAL Generalist Agent was not. A paired delta between them therefore blends the effect of harness architecture with the effect of benchmark-specific tuning effort, and we cannot separate the two from published tables. This is the strongest objection to the interaction result and we do not have an answer to it. It is also a reason to expect the effect to be largest exactly where we find it largest, which is an alternative explanation of Table Table 4 that the data cannot rule out.

Cost is a partial confound, but not the explanation. A scaffold can buy accuracy with tokens, and HAL reports dollar cost beside every accuracy, so this is checkable. Across all 63 pairs the correlation between the paired accuracy difference and the log cost ratio of the better to the worse scaffold is 0.03, and in 27 of 63 pairs the better scaffold was also the cheaper one. On SWE-bench Verified Mini alone the correlation is higher at 0.57, so cost does explain part of the effect there — but not the largest case: o3 Medium gains 46 points under SWE-Agent while spending 17\% less money. A harness that converts 0.0\% into 46.0\% for less money is not a budget effect.

On benchmark overlap. The numerator in Table Table 2 is measured on SWE-bench Lite, Terminal-Bench 2, SWE-bench Pro, SWE-bench Verified and WeaveBench; the HAL cells are measured on SWE-bench Verified Mini, τ-bench Airline, CORE-Bench, AssistantBench, GAIA, OnlineMind2Web, ScienceAgentBench, SciCode and USACO. The intersection of those two sets is empty — an earlier draft of this paper described them as “overlapping but not identical,” which was simply wrong. This is the main reason we now lead with the paired comparison of Table Table 3 rather than with a cross-source comparison: within Table Table 3 both sides come from identical runs, so the objection does not arise.

Which cells are included

We state the inclusion rule because an earlier draft did not. From the per-model tables of [74] we take every benchmark–scaffold cell retaining at least four frontier families after effort-collapsing, which yields fifteen cells; and every model that appears under two scaffolds on the same benchmark, which yields 63 pairs across seven benchmarks. No cell meeting those rules is excluded. An earlier draft reported eleven cells selected by an unstated rule, which silently dropped at least two eligible ones.

What survives all of this is narrower than what the previous three drafts of this paper claimed, and we think it is worth more. The comparison the field organised itself around is computable, it has been computable since 2025, the cost of computing it was an afternoon of arithmetic on public tables, and the answer is that neither side dominates. The 40,000 factorial that Section 8 prices remains the way to settle it properly; what an afternoon buys is the knowledge that the premise was never checked. {Boundary conditions}

Three further findings bound the belief. Two survive regrading intact; the first is itself E4 under our rule and we flag it as such rather than leaning on it.

First, matched conditions shrink reported spreads sharply. On a successor version of the same terminal benchmark, harness spreads reportedly collapse to between 0.2 and 8.1 points once reasoning effort and submission dates are matched [64]. This figure is E4 under our rule and we use it only as a hypothesis-generator, not as evidence: it indicates that leaderboard spreads substantially reflect unmatched configuration, which is exactly what a disclosure standard would fix.

Second, and this is the strongest result in the whole corpus on the question, harness churn does not imply harness improvement. A controlled longitudinal study fixed the model and varied only the harness across 35 sequential releases of one command-line agent, evaluated on 50 stratified benchmark tasks [21]. Release velocity across five open-source harnesses exceeded two releases per day. Despite that, there was no statistically significant improvement in resolve rate for a fixed model, and later versions consumed nearly double the tokens and tool calls. This is a primary, controlled, model-fixed design, and it is a null result. E2

Third, benchmark-oriented optimisation does not transfer. Checkpoints post-trained on one benchmark's trajectories show little cross-task transfer, and optimisation for that benchmark yields limited or no gains on an independent suite [135]. Applied to harnesses rather than weights, the optimisation literature says the same thing: the best harness varies across domains, instances, and base models [175]. E2

6.5Model–harness co-evolution

Harness findings have a shelf life, which compounds the measurement problem. The clearest demonstration is a reversal within one research line: two successive systems won by adding hierarchy and generalist–specialist decomposition to computer-use agents [3, 4], and the third won by abandoning further hierarchy for scaling multiple rollouts with a behaviour-narrative judge, surpassing human-level performance on its benchmark [49]. The architectural answer changed because the models underneath changed. No study in this corpus measures how harness gain shrinks, grows, or flips across model generations, so even a well-measured ratio would need a date attached. E2

6.6The evidence map

Table Table 5 exposes two patterns invisible in any single paper, and one row whose sign is now negative. The field's organising belief is graded E3: evidence exists, it is observational rather than controlled, and it runs against the direction claimed (Section 6.4). Its deployment maturity is M3, meaning it is already shaping engineering decisions in production teams. A belief at M3 whose only available evidence points the wrong way is the single most consequential entry in this table.

The first pattern is the maturity–evidence inversion in the state and context facets. Four claims are graded M4, meaning production defaults in widely deployed harnesses, while their evidence is E3 or E4. Compaction policy, cross-session artifacts, initializer agents, and the shape of context degradation are all running in front of large user populations on the strength of engineering reports. This is not a criticism of those reports, which are specific and candid. It identifies the highest-value controlled experiments available to the field: the interventions whose effects are already assumed are the ones nobody has isolated. The counter-evidence that naive episodic memory harms long-horizon reliability [77] shows the direction of such effects is not safely assumed.

The second pattern runs the other way. Three claims are graded M0 with E2 evidence: delegated-network domination, the uncontrolled scaffold-ownership axis, and absent external verifiability. These are established results that have not reached practice, and they define a transfer problem rather than a research problem.

7Evaluation and Its Validity Threats

7.1Benchmark families

Two entries in Table Table 6 deserve emphasis because they measure the harness directly rather than through it. The scaffold-compliance benchmark asks whether agents follow scaffold-specified instructions when constraints are heterogeneous and persist across interactions, using 34 environments and 217 tasks under three scaffold types with 7,098 objective checklist items, and it finds a systematic gap between task-solving and scaffold-aware compliance [39]. An agent can succeed while ignoring the harness, which is precisely the failure mode that makes harness-level guarantees unreliable. The harness-construction benchmark shifts the unit of evaluation from task outputs to runnable infrastructure, with creation and evolution stages scored on both capability and execution-token efficiency [23].

A validity check on the benchmark families themselves is worth recording. An automated data engine that reverse-engineers tasks from 80,870 in-the-wild terminal recordings finds its scores correlate only weakly with an expert-curated terminal benchmark at a Pearson coefficient of 0.20 [28]. Expert-curated and in-the-wild terminal capability are therefore measuring substantially different constructs, and any claim about “terminal capability” resting on one of them should say which.

7.2The double measurement confound

Agent benchmark scores are interpretable as model capability only under conditions that are rarely met, and the cleanest formalisation names two simultaneous failures: execution-critical decisions are performed by a fixed scaffold rather than the model, while the scorer evaluates outputs by criteria that may not reflect task correctness [182]. The proposed audit-and-repair protocol transfers execution-critical decisions from scaffold to model, replaces shape-based evaluation with seeded ground-truth scoring, and reports worst-case and tail-risk metrics instead of the mean alone. Applying it turned a nearly flat leaderboard into a reliability spectrum. Its two general conclusions are the ones to carry forward: scorer validity is benchmark-specific, and scaffold ownership is an uncontrolled axis wherever they probed it.

7.3Outcome-only scoring and log analysis

Pass-or-fail reporting threatens validity in three distinct ways, and log analysis is the only available remedy. Scores may be inflated or deflated by shortcuts and benchmark artifacts; benchmark performance may fail to predict real-world utility because of scaffold limitations and recurring failure modes; and capability scores can conceal dangerous or catastrophic actions [79]. Demonstrated on one airline-domain benchmark, log analysis found pass5 performance had been under-elicited by nearly 50% and surfaced deployment failure modes invisible to outcome metrics.

Standardised infrastructure makes the point at scale. A leaderboard effort built an evaluation harness orchestrating parallel runs across hundreds of virtual machines, validated with 21,730 rollouts across nine models and nine benchmarks at a cost of roughly $40,000, and analysed along three dimensions of models, scaffolds, and benchmarks [74]. Two classes of finding emerged that outcome metrics cannot produce. Higher reasoning effort reduced accuracy in the majority of runs, which inverts a common assumption. And model-aided log inspection found agents searching for the benchmark on a model hub instead of solving the task, and misusing credit cards in booking tasks. All logs, comprising 2.5 billion tokens, were released.

The agenda these results serve was set in 2024 by a paper whose four diagnoses still structure the area [75]: narrow accuracy focus makes state-of-the-art agents needlessly complex and costly and has led to mistaken conclusions about the sources of accuracy gains; the benchmarking needs of model developers and downstream developers are conflated; many benchmarks have inadequate or absent holdout sets, producing agents that take shortcuts and overfit; and non-standardised practice produces pervasive irreproducibility.

7.4Reliability beyond the mean

Capability and reliability are different properties that diverge systematically as task duration grows, and single-attempt metrics on short tasks are structurally blind to the divergence. A reliability framework contributes four purpose-built metrics, namely a reliability decay curve over duration, a variance amplification factor, a graceful degradation score for partial completion, and a meltdown onset point detected via sliding-window entropy over tool-call sequences, evaluated across 396 tasks and 23,392 episodes [77]. Three of its five findings are counter-intuitive and bear directly on harness design. High variance amplification is a capability signature rather than an instability signature. Capability and reliability rankings diverge substantially, with multi-rank inversions between medium and very-long horizons. And memory scaffolds universally hurt long-horizon graceful degradation across all ten models tested, which is strong evidence against naive episodic memory as a reliability intervention.

A complementary framework grounded in safety-critical engineering supplies twelve metrics across consistency, robustness, predictability, and safety, and finds that recent capability gains have produced only small reliability improvements across 15 models and two benchmarks [120]. The reliability metric that first exposed this, passk, came from a tool–agent–user benchmark reporting that state-of-the-art function-calling agents succeeded on under half of tasks and were highly inconsistent across repeats [173]. Peak capability and reliable capability are different quantities, and only the second is deployable.

7.5Cost

Cost reporting remains optional and inconsistent, which makes efficiency claims hard to compare. Pareto reporting has spread since the 2024 critique [75], but search cost in the harness-optimisation literature is reported unevenly: one leading method reports the task model's context tokens rather than total proposer compute [83]. A rubric that downgrades unreported cost therefore downgrades much of Section 10, which is itself a finding about the field rather than about any individual paper.

Three mechanisms reduce cost without accuracy loss, and all three are harness interventions. Early termination driven by structured experience from prior executions cut total cost by 19% to 55% with at most 0.2% resolution-rate loss [51]. Temporal routing lets a cheap model run a few exploratory turns, reads the resulting partial trajectory, and only then decides whether to escalate, with a Bayes-optimality theorem showing that conditioning on the partial trajectory never harms routing and strictly helps when exploration is informative [137]. Heterogeneous agent teams with mixed deployment occupy the cost–accuracy frontier, improving accuracy by up to 44% over cost-equivalent homogeneous teams or matching the strongest homogeneous team at up to twelvefold lower per-task cost [67]. Evaluation cost is itself reducible: restricting new evaluations to tasks with intermediate historical pass rates cuts task count by 44% to 70% while preserving rank fidelity, exploiting the asymmetry that absolute score prediction degrades under scaffold-driven distribution shift while rank-order prediction stays stable [45].

8Specifying the Missing Measurement

8.1Scope of this section, and whose work it is

Everything in this section is future work, and the two instruments it specifies were proposed first by Zhang et al. [183], who introduced both a harness disclosure standard and a variance-decomposition protocol as part of defending the thesis. We are not claiming either. What we add is detail that the audit makes possible: which disclosure fields have been shown to move results and which are already free to report, and what the minimum viable factorial actually costs now that Section 6.3 has established there is no denominator to compare against.

The motivation is no longer rhetorical. Section Section 6.3 showed that the comparison the field makes cannot be computed from controlled evidence, and Section Section 7 shows why: a leaderboard row attributing a score to a model is not interpretable, because the score belongs to a system whose other components are undisclosed. The remedy is not a better benchmark. It is a reporting standard plus one factorial experiment, in the same sense that model cards and datasheets became reporting standards for models and datasets.

8.2A twelve-field disclosure record

Seven of the twelve fields are marked as commonly missing, and each omission corresponds to a documented measurement failure rather than to a hypothetical one. Reasoning effort is marked because higher effort reduced accuracy in the majority of runs in the largest standardised study [74], so a comparison that leaves it unstated is uninterpretable. Harness version is marked because 35 sequential releases of one harness changed token consumption nearly twofold [21]. Repeats are marked because single-attempt scores conceal the capability–reliability divergence of Section 7 [77, 173]. The card is deliberately shorter than a full systems description: it is the set of fields that have been shown to move results, not an inventory of everything a harness contains.

8.3The minimum viable factorial

Disclosure alone does not attribute variance, so we specify the experiment that would. Let m index models, h index harnesses, b index benchmark instances, and r index repeated runs. The minimum viable design is a factorial over m × h for fixed b, with r ≥ 5:

ymhbr  =  μ  +  αm  +  βh  +  (αβ)mh  +  γb  +  εmhbr,
where the quantity of interest is not any single coefficient but the variance components σ2_α (model), σ2_β (harness), σ2αβ (interaction), and σ2_ε (run-to-run noise). This design is what would settle the question Section 6.3 leaves open. The belief under audit corresponds to the hypothesis σ2_β > σ2_α in the long-horizon regime; the null it must beat is σ2_β σ2_α, which the surviving controlled evidence is equally consistent with. The co-evolution result of Section 6 predicts a non-negligible σ2αβ either way, which is the formal statement of “the best harness depends on the model”. We make no prediction about which hypothesis wins, and we note that a result in either direction is publishable and useful.

Three design requirements follow from the failures catalogued earlier. Reasoning effort must be matched across cells, or the design silently confounds it with harness [64, 74]. Harness versions must be pinned, since the same name denotes different systems weeks apart [21]. And runs must be repeated, because reporting only the mean hides the tail behaviour that distinguishes systems [182, 77]. A minimum viable instance would be four models by four harnesses by one benchmark by five repeats, which is 80 cells; existing standardised infrastructure has demonstrated this is tractable at roughly $40,000 for a larger design, and task-subsetting reduces it further while preserving rankings [74, 45].

No such factorial dataset currently exists. The largest standardised effort analyses models, scaffolds, and benchmarks but relies on single runs without statistical validation [74], and the position paper that proposes variance decomposition acknowledges its own factorial is small [183]. Producing it is, in our assessment, the single highest-value empirical contribution available in this area.

8.4What adoption costs

A disclosure standard that is expensive to follow will not be followed, so it is worth being concrete. Eleven of the twelve fields are already known to whoever ran the experiment and cost only the effort to write them down. The twelfth, repeated runs, multiplies compute by k, and that is a real cost. We suggest the pragmatic compromise that single-run results remain publishable when labelled as such, while any comparative claim between systems requires k ≥ 5 and reports passk. This mirrors the distinction the reliability literature draws between measuring capability and measuring deployability [120, 77].

9Security and Governance of the Harness

9.1The structural vulnerability

Language models operate on text and have no formal way to distinguish instructions from data, which is the unfixed root of the entire area [35]. Indirect prompt injection exploits it by embedding instructions in content the agent retrieves, so an external attacker can act on the user's behalf. The first benchmark for this found a widely used agent configuration vulnerable 24% of the time, nearly doubling when attacker instructions were reinforced [180]. A dynamic successor made evaluation extensible rather than static, seeding an environment with 97 realistic tasks and 629 security test cases, and found the setting hard for both sides: strong models fail many tasks even unattacked, and existing attacks break some security properties but not all [35]. E1 M4

9.2Defence by design, and its limits

The only design-level guarantee in the corpus builds a protective system layer drawing on control-flow integrity, access control, and information-flow control: control and data flow are extracted from the trusted query so untrusted data retrieved later cannot influence program flow, and capabilities attached to every value block exfiltration over unauthorised flows at tool-call time [34]. It solves 77% of a security benchmark's tasks with provable security against 84% for an undefended system, which quantifies the security tax rather than hiding it.

Those guarantees do not compose. In hierarchical systems where agents invoke other agents as tools, a working attack succeeds even though every constituent agent individually runs the defence, because untrusted data can be reinterpreted as trusted input by a downstream agent [115]. The remedy preserves provenance across agent boundaries by separating trusted instructions from untrusted data on a distinct channel, reducing attack success to zero from 12.9% undefended, at a utility cost that shrinks as models improve. Composition failure across trust boundaries is a general pattern in this corpus rather than a quirk of one defence. E2 M1

9.3The harness as the attack surface

The most consequential security result for harness design is that the harness itself can defeat the industry's main structural defence. Both standard protections, namely a model trained to refuse unsafe instructions and a separate permission reviewer, depend on role labels being faithful; but the harness constructs the context for every model invocation and can thereby elevate low-privilege content to a higher instruction level [54]. The canonical demonstration is worth stating in full because it is not an exotic exploit. A coding agent read a repository, identified a plaintext backdoor, and refused to start the server. It then delegated the identical step to a helper agent, which executed it immediately, because the harness had written the sub-agent's task into the user slot, which is the obvious engineering choice for a delegation feature. In one transcript a permission reviewer correctly flagged a command as high risk and approved it anyway, reasoning that the user had explicitly approved this exact action, when the user had typed nothing. Nothing is forged and no one is deceived; the safety layer approves a consent that was never given. This is a confused-deputy provenance failure, and the attacks achieved all 13 objectives across all six coding-agent harnesses tested, including under automatic permission review. The authors define the problem without offering a fix, and the preconditions are real: the agent must be pointed at attacker-controlled files, and the exploit is probabilistic. E2 M4

The harness configuration layer is also a software supply chain. A study of 3,171 public repositories treating repository instructions, skills, hooks, tool-server declarations, and sub-agent definitions as distributed artifacts found unpinned server package declarations in 9.8% of setups, broad execution grants in 2.5%, and broad skill tool preapproval in 3.8%, with the union covering 15.4% and rising to 17.9% including conformance issues [73]. Among setups that configure a tool server at all, 24.5% contain an unpinned declaration. The remedies are mundane and available: pin dependencies, review execution preapproval, check component conformance before distribution. E2 M3

9.4The protocol layer

Four independent studies of the dominant tool protocol reach consistent conclusions, which is why this claim is graded E1. An empirical study of 1,899 open-source servers found eight distinct vulnerability classes of which only three overlap traditional software vulnerabilities, with 7.2% of servers containing general vulnerabilities and 5.5% exhibiting protocol-specific tool poisoning [53]. A systematic benchmark enumerating 17 attack types across four surfaces found all four surfaces yield successful compromises, with current protections averaging under 30% success [170]. Treating servers as active threat actors yields twelve attack categories with working proofs of concept, and the two findings that matter operationally are that malicious servers can be generated at near-zero cost and that state-of-the-art scanners fail to detect them [185]. And an identity analysis shows that trusting servers without authenticating the caller is fundamentally insecure, since most servers rely on persistent authorisation state that permits invocation after one initial approval regardless of who calls [58].

The most actionable framing organises defences by enforcement responsibility rather than by attack, mapping threats across six protocol layers and identifying primary and secondary defence points. Its coverage analysis finds existing defences uneven and predominantly tool-centric, with persistent gaps at the host orchestration, transport, and supply-chain layers, and concludes that many weaknesses stem from architectural misalignment rather than isolated implementation flaws [125]. A systematisation of knowledge adds the conceptual axis that matters most: in agentic systems the boundary between an epistemic error and a security breach dissolves, because a hallucination now causes an unauthorised action [47]. E1 M4

9.5Measuring defences by layer

Attack success rate is the wrong metric, because it cannot distinguish a model recognition failure from an agent-layer block and therefore cannot indicate which layer to strengthen [178]. A layer-resolved alternative captures raw context at the gateway and replaces the single rate with four classes, namely semantic refusal, semantic breach, interception, and penetration. Its accompanying model identifies the envelope layer, meaning the channel through which content enters the agent, as a defence surface, and ablations across agent-to-agent, tool, and memory channels show that simply annotating untrusted content markedly improves recognition of malicious content. This is a cheap intervention with a measured effect, and it belongs in the Observation facet as much as in security. E2 M1

10Automated Harness Optimisation

10.1Why the field pursued this

The belief audited in Section 6 did not need to be true to be productive. It was enough that practitioners believed the harness carried the performance and that harnesses were written by hand: twenty-one sources in this corpus, almost all from 2026, follow that inference to its conclusion and try to optimise the harness automatically. We review them here as a research programme in their own right, noting that their internal evidence is considerably better than the evidence for the premise that motivated them, since most report controlled before-and-after comparisons with the model held fixed. They share one premise: execution traces are a training signal, so failures diagnosed from trajectories can become edits to prompts, tools, context policy, and control logic. The work divides into search over harness code, learned editors and controllers, just-in-time generation, distillation back into weights, and recursive self-improvement.

10.2Search over harness code

The reference method diagnoses why generic text optimisers fail at this task: they compress feedback too aggressively, being memoryless, conditioned only on scalar scores, or restricted to short templates [83]. It instead gives an agentic proposer filesystem access to the source code, scores, and execution traces of all prior candidates, improving over a state-of-the-art context-management system by 7.7 points while using fourfold fewer context tokens, and beating every reported harness for one model on a terminal benchmark.

The observability-driven variant contributes the idea most worth transferring. It identifies three obstacles to automation, namely a heterogeneous action space across editable components, voluminous trajectories that bury actionable signal, and edits whose effects are hard to attribute, and matches each with an observability pillar: file-level representation of every editable component so the action space is explicit and revertible; trajectories distilled into a layered drill-down evidence corpus; and every edit paired with a self-declared prediction, later verified against the next round's outcomes [85]. That last mechanism turns each edit into a falsifiable contract, which is what prevents evolution collapsing into trial and error. Ten iterations lifted a terminal benchmark from 69.7% to 77.0%, above a human-designed reference, and the frozen harness transferred to a different benchmark with 12% fewer tokens and gained 5.1 to 10.1 points across three other model families, which is evidence the evolved components encode general engineering experience rather than benchmark-specific tuning. E2 M2

A third method formulates harness improvement as offline learning from mini-batch failure signals and reports the most transferable design guidance in this area: effective optimisation requires deep debugging rather than shallow reflection, targeted modifications rather than unconstrained editing, and generalisation-aware selection rather than trajectory-specific repair [110]. The cleanest proof of concept comes from a game setting, where 78% of one model's losses were attributed to illegal moves rather than strategic error; a few rounds of iterative code refinement against environment feedback produced a harness preventing all illegal moves across 145 games, letting the smaller model outperform a larger one, and pushed further, generating the entire policy as code and removing the model from decision time altogether [90]. E1 M2

10.3Learned editors, controllers, and generators

Four variants make the optimiser itself learned rather than prompted. A failure-conditioned editor post-trains a dedicated 9-billion-parameter harness engineer with online reinforcement learning so that its edits are optimised for realised task success, with same-batch reruns of the frozen target providing outcome rewards so only the engineer updates [132]. An interface-adaptation method targets deterministic rule-governed domains where failures stem from model–environment interface mismatch, converting recurring failures into reusable interventions and then freezing for evaluation, reportedly exceeding two of the methods above while using 8.5% and 68.4% fewer optimisation tokens [169]. A just-in-time generator synthesises task-adaptive harnesses on the fly for arbitrary off-the-shelf models under a fixed four-module protocol, positioning harness intelligence as trainable, transferable, and orthogonal to model scaling [70]. And a foundry composes typed harness primitives through a substitution algebra across nine orthogonal dimensions, closing the harness–model loop so trajectories become both harness updates and model training signal, with gains largest where baselines are lowest [32].

Two further directions change what the optimisation produces. Harness distillation observes that gains are normally tied to the harness at deployment, and since the best harness varies by domain, instance, and model, it uses an optimised harness as training-time guidance only and distils the induced behaviours into weights so the specialised harness can be removed; base macro-average success rose from 23.3% to 44.3%, exceeding the 41.7% reached with the harness still attached [175]. Recursive harnessing targets open-world settings where test-time tasks are unseen so no ground-truth verifier exists to guide online edits, using a contrastive propose–probe–compose step that needs none, plus a second level of recursion that accumulates experience across tasks [166]. Self-improvement without external help is also demonstrated: weakness mining from execution traces, minimal harness proposals tied to those failures, and validation gated on regression testing improved both held-in and held-out pass rates across all nine model–benchmark combinations tested [184]. E1 M1

10.4The reality check

The benchmark that evaluates harness construction directly is the necessary corrective. Across six creator models, four domains, five downstream benchmarks, and 2,207 instances with hidden evaluation tasks withheld, generated harnesses remain substantially behind mature human-engineered references on code and on search and research, while matching or exceeding them on writing and machine -learning experimentation, with large variation in execution cost [23]. Evolution produced some gains, but they were unstable and transferred only partially, and depended strongly on which model executed the harness. A companion result identifies why evolution is hard: behaviour localisation is the bottleneck, because before anyone, human or agent, can change a behaviour they must find every code location implementing it, and production harnesses are large, tightly coupled, and behaviourally distributed across files, functions, execution stages, and state transitions [147]. E2 M1

A practitioner inventory supplies the calibration that research papers omit: across nine open-source self-improving frameworks, grouped by whether they change prompts, memory and skills, tools, or the workflow, nothing shipping retrains weights in production, and several published systems are research-grade rather than production-ready [46]. E4 M2

11Open Problems and Research Agenda

P1. Neither side of the comparison has an interval.

Section Section 6.4 computes both sides from single runs. We bound the sampling error analytically from each benchmark's task count, which is why we report that 31 of 63 paired deltas exceed noise rather than all of them, but an analytic bound is not a substitute for repeats. The complementary study already runs five seeds at temperature zero across 15 models [120] and reports accuracy only graphically. The cheapest high-value task in this whole agenda is therefore not a new experiment but a re-analysis: recompute both sides from released per-seed data and publish intervals. Until that exists, the orders of magnitude are established and the ratio is not.

P1b. The interaction is confounded with tuning effort.

Table Table 4 cannot distinguish harness architecture from benchmark-specific tuning, because the scaffolds that do best on a benchmark are generally the ones built for it. Separating the two requires evaluating one purpose-built and one generic scaffold on a benchmark neither was tuned for, which no published campaign has done and which costs far less than the full factorial of P3.

P2. Taxonomy fragmentation.

Three formal taxonomies and two informal decompositions coexist [63, 118, 50, 186, 57]. The cost is not aesthetic: work on compaction, tool schemas, sandboxing, and tracing addresses one system through four disjoint bibliographies. We deliberately did not add a sixth (Section 1.1). The field needs consolidation by adoption, not by further proposal.

P3. No factorial model × harness dataset exists.

This is P1's constructive form. Every quantitative claim about relative model and harness contribution currently rests on practitioner reports or small single-factor studies. The protocol in Section 8.3 is specified; the dataset is not built. The largest standardised effort relies on single runs without statistical validation [74]. This is the highest-value empirical gap in the area.

P4. Harness effects have an unmeasured shelf life.

The optimal harness co-evolves with model capability, demonstrated by a reversal in which added hierarchy helped two model generations and was then superseded by rollout scaling [3, 4, 49]. No study measures how harness gain shrinks, grows, or flips across generations. Any maturity scorecard for harness techniques therefore needs a time dimension, and published harness results need an expiry date.

P5. The maturity–evidence inversion in state and context.

Compaction policy, cross-session artifacts, initializer agents, and context-degradation shape are production defaults supported by vendor reports [13, 64, 41]. They are simultaneously the mechanisms most users depend on and the least controlled. The counter-evidence that naive episodic memory harms long-horizon reliability [77] shows the direction of such effects is not safely assumed.

P6. Cost and search cost are reported inconsistently.

Harness optimisation papers report proposer compute unevenly [83], and cost regressions hide easily when nobody reports cost: later releases of one harness nearly doubled token consumption without quality gain [21]. Any evidence rubric that downgrades unreported cost will downgrade much of the 2026 optimisation literature, which is a finding about reporting norms.

P7. Trace poisoning against self-modifying harnesses.

This risk is created by the field's own methods and is, to our knowledge, unstudied. Harness optimisers rewrite harness code from execution traces [83, 85, 132, 110]. An attacker who can influence traces can therefore influence harness edits, which persist across runs and users in a way a single injected instruction does not. The adjacent empirical results make the concern concrete: the harness already defeats the instruction hierarchy by relabelling content [54], and configuration artifacts already travel through an unvetted supply chain [73]. The defensive primitive that would help, namely falsifiable prediction-checked edits, already exists [85]; it has not been evaluated adversarially.

P8. External verifiability is absent everywhere.

Mature harnesses converge on append-only replayable session records, yet a tamper-evident record an outside party can check without trusting the runtime appears in none of the harnesses examined at source level [66]. Internal tracing serves debugging; audit needs something stronger. For regulated and provenance-sensitive deployments this is a blocking gap, and the formal-policy and compliance-verification lines are the natural place to close it [101, 72].

P9. Generalisation of optimised harnesses, and benchmark-oriented overfitting.

One method transfers a harness across five held-out models [83], while others report that the best harness varies across domains, instances, and base models [175], and transfer is unstable in the one benchmark built to test it [23]. Over-fitting harnesses to benchmarks is the agent-era analogue of test-set overfitting, and early evidence for it already exists in the weights setting [135]. Scaffold-compliance measurement [39] and in-the-wild benchmark construction [28] are the most promising instruments for detecting it.

12Conclusion

We set out to survey the evidence for the field's organising belief about agent harnesses, found that the comparison the belief rests on had never been computed, and computed it. Applying a consistent evidence scale to a 191-source corpus leaves four controlled same-model harness interventions moving primary-benchmark scores by 7.0, 7.3, 10.1, and 28.9 percentage points across three distinct benchmarks. Both sides of the comparison then turn out to be available from one standardised evaluation campaign already cited nine times in this paper, measured on identical runs: 63 paired model×scaffold comparisons give a median harness effect of 9.0 points, and fifteen fixed-scaffold frontier cells give a median model-side dispersion of 6.9 points. On the evidence now available the two are the same order of magnitude, and the harness is neither the binding constraint nor a second-order concern.

The result we did not expect, and the one we think matters most, is that the question is not well-posed as a contest. Model-side dispersion depends on which harness is held fixed — by a median factor of 1.8× across six benchmarks, and by 4.0× on SWE-bench Verified Mini, where seven frontier families sit within one standard error of each other under a purpose-built scaffold and span 46 points under a generic one. On that benchmark the harness does not merely outweigh the model; it sets whether the model matters at all. Asking which factor dominates in general is asking for a main effect in the presence of a large interaction.

We hold all of this at the strength the data supports and no further. A paired treatment effect and a population dispersion are different kinds of quantity. Every cell is a single run, and we bound sampling error analytically rather than observing it. Scaffold–model pairings are self-selected, so the interaction is confounded with tuning effort, and we have no way to separate them from published tables. Cost explains part of the effect on one benchmark, though across all 63 pairs the correlation is 0.03 and in 27 of them the better scaffold was the cheaper one. What survives every one of those caveats is a claim about orders of magnitude, a claim about interaction, and a claim about cost: the measurement the field treated as prohibitive was an afternoon of arithmetic on tables published in 2025.

The harness still demonstrably matters, and nothing here says otherwise. A bounded file viewer and an LM-friendly command surface moved a benchmark by 7 points with weights untouched [171]; externalising task state and auditing it independently moved another by 28.9 [92]; ten rounds of trace-driven optimisation moved a third by 7.3 [85]. The claim that fails is the comparative one, and it fails in a specific and recoverable way: nobody had put the two quantities in the same table.

The audit produced a second finding we consider more durable than the computation, because it does not depend on any single source. The mechanisms most users depend on are the least controlled. Context compaction, cross-session artifacts, and initializer agents are production defaults in widely deployed harnesses, graded M4, and their evidence is observational or vendor-reported, graded E3 or E4. One of them has counter-evidence: naive episodic memory measurably harms long-horizon reliability across ten models [77]. A field that has shipped a mechanism to millions of users without isolating its effect has a research agenda handed to it, and this inversion is where we would start.

Two cautions about our own work close the paper. Our corpus is a convenience sample pooled from three literature maps, so a source absent from all three is absent here, and our grading was applied by one reader without an inter-annotator statistic; both are stated in Section 2 rather than mitigated. And the central computation is arithmetic on someone else's tables, which means the most useful thing a reader can do with this paper is disagree with a cell and recompute. We have made that as easy as we can.
